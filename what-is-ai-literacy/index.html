What is AI literacy, and why does a hospitality team need it?

Michael J. Goldrich · September 2026

AI literacy is the ability to use AI tools well in your own role: knowing what they do, what they get wrong, which decisions they should support and which they should never make, and running a routine with them that saves time every week. It is a working skill, not a course, and it is the subject of The AI Literacy Playbook for Hoteliers, my 2025 book and the curriculum behind Vivander Advisors' training programs. Hospitality teams need it because the tools are already on their desks, nobody trained them, and the result is quiet non-use: licenses bought, a few people experimenting, everyone else waiting.

AI literacy is not a course. It is a mindset shift. Once that shift happens, real change becomes possible. Before it, you are buying licenses.

What does an AI-literate person know?

Four things.

How the tools work, at the level needed to trust or distrust an output. Answers are sampled. The tool fills gaps confidently. Context decides quality more than the prompt does.

What the tools get wrong in their own domain. Rates. Availability. Policies. Tone. Anything that changed recently.

Which decisions the tool should support and which it should not touch. A draft reply to a wedding inquiry, yes. Rate decisions with no human in the loop, no.

And a routine. A task from their own job that the tool now does faster, with a check built in.

Why is generic training not enough?

Because a revenue manager and a front-office supervisor need different routines on different tools with different failure modes. Generic courses teach prompting on essays and code. A hotel team needs to see the comp-set summary the tool drafted and find where it is wrong, turn a group inquiry into a proposal in the property's voice, and write the one-page usage policy the team will actually follow.

I walked into an AHLA T100 session with a single property flash report. Nothing proprietary. Then I showed what NotebookLM could do with it. Eleven different outputs from one document. The Zoom room went quiet. That silence is the moment literacy starts.

What did the research find?

The hospitality industry foundation's 2026 report Case Study: The Future Talent Pipeline, which I authored, found hospitality students rating their academic programs' preparation for AI at 2.78 out of 5, and learning the tools by experimenting on their own. Employers inherit that gap on day one. The earlier report in the series found leaders had rarely decided what a recovered hour was for. Literacy inside the company closes both gaps.

What does AI literacy have to do with AI visibility?

They are the two halves of the foundation. Visibility is whether AI recommends the business. Literacy is whether the team can keep it that way: reading the audit, publishing the facts, answering the questions, checking the re-tests. A visibility fix handed to a team with no literacy decays. The HFTP AI Collective, which I co-chair, is building an industry AI certificate on the same premise.

How long does it take?

A half-day workshop establishes the baseline for up to 25 people. Full days add role tracks. Five-week and eleven-week programs build routines with assignments on real work between sessions. Agent Builder Sessions, up to two hours, leave each person with a working AI agent system on their own laptop. Every program ends with a scorecard and a named owner.

Where this is going

Your hotel staff will become AI supervisors. Most hospitality executives are not ready for what that means. The employee who learns to orchestrate agents becomes the most valuable person in the building, and that person is not going to be hired. They are going to be trained, or they are going to leave for somewhere that trains them.

Treat AI competency like spreadsheet skills. Nobody hires a revenue manager who cannot use Excel. Within two years nobody will hire one who cannot brief an agent.
