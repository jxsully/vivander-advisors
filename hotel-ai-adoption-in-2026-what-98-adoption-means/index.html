Hotel AI Adoption in 2026: What 98% Adoption Means

Michael J. Goldrich · January 31, 2026

The hospitality industry has reached an inflection point with hotel artificial intelligence adoption. According to the Wyndham Hotels & Resorts 2026 Hotel Owner Trends Report, conducted in partnership with Wakefield Research, 98% of hotel owners and property developers have already incorporated AI into their businesses. The era of asking “should we adopt AI?” has ended. The question now centers on something far more complex: how do organizations move from experimentation to execution at scale?

This shift represents more than a technology upgrade. It signals a structural transformation in how hospitality businesses operate, compete, and create value for guests. And within this transformation lies a profound challenge that the data makes clear: 73% of hoteliers want to expand their AI capabilities but feel overwhelmed and uncertain about where to begin.

The gap between adoption and effective implementation has become the defining challenge for hospitality leadership in 2026.

What Is Changing in Hospitality AI Adoption

The survey of 325 hotel owners and property developers across the United States, Canada, and the Caribbean reveals a market that has moved decisively beyond the pilot phase. Nearly all respondents (98%) have started incorporating AI into their hotel businesses. The breakdown tells a more nuanced story.

Thirty-two percent have incorporated AI into most aspects of their operations. Forty-two percent have deployed AI in some areas with plans to integrate more. Twenty-three percent have recently started their AI journey. Only 2% have yet to begin.

The applications driving this adoption cluster around operational efficiency (64%), energy efficiency (54%), and revenue optimization (53%). Construction planning follows at 42%. Guest experience enhancement, in-room AI features, and personalization currently represent smaller portions of deployment at 11%, 10%, and 9% respectively.

What makes 2026 different from previous years becomes apparent in where owners plan to expand. Construction planning leads future adoption plans at 61%, suggesting that AI’s role in development, permitting, and space planning represents the next frontier for the industry.

Why This Matters for Hospitality Organizations & AI Adoption

The data reveals a paradox at the heart of hotel AI adoption. Near-universal adoption coexists with widespread uncertainty about expansion. This paradox illuminates something important about organizational readiness.

Technology adoption does not equal organizational capability.

The barriers keeping owners from expanding their AI deployments reflect this reality. Data privacy and security concerns top the list at 46%. Costs of AI investments follow at 42%. Integration challenges with legacy systems rank third at 40%. Training requirements (38%), staff resistance (38%), and lack of understanding about AI applications (36%) round out the primary obstacles.

These barriers share a common thread. They are organizational challenges, rather than technical ones. Security concerns reflect governance gaps. Integration difficulties point to infrastructure debt. Training needs and staff resistance indicate workforce readiness gaps. Uncertainty about AI applications suggests strategic clarity has lagged behind tactical deployment.

The industry sentiment remains optimistic despite these challenges. Ninety percent of hotel owners express optimism about 2026, and 94% feel optimistic about the next five years. Seventy-nine percent plan to expand their portfolios over that period. This confidence suggests that leaders recognize the opportunity even as they grapple with the complexity of seizing it.

What This Means for Work in Hospitality & AI Adoption

The nature of work within hospitality organizations is evolving alongside AI adoption. The survey findings illuminate several dimensions of this evolution.

Human oversight remains central to AI deployment. When asked about comfort with AI making operational decisions, 40% of respondents expressed complete comfort even without human oversight. Another 57% indicated comfort with human oversight in place. Only 3% reported discomfort even with oversight.

This distribution suggests that hospitality leaders see AI as a capable partner for certain decisions while maintaining human judgment as essential for others. The work of hospitality professionals is shifting from execution toward supervision, exception handling, and judgment-intensive activities.

Staffing remains the top capital investment priority for 2026, cited by 24% of respondents. Marketing and sales improvements (20%), property improvements (20%), and technology investments (19%) follow closely. The fact that increased staffing leads technology investment in capital allocation priorities indicates that human capability remains central to competitive advantage.

The obstacles data reinforces this point. Talent shortages and skill gaps rank as the second-highest obstacle facing hotel businesses this year at 35%, trailing only high operating costs at 37%. Rising competition (34%), cybersecurity threats (33%), and supply chain issues (32%) complete the top five concerns.

Work in hospitality is becoming less about completing tasks and more about orchestrating outcomes. The front desk agent who once processed check-ins now oversees automated systems while handling exceptions and delivering personalized service moments that AI cannot replicate. The revenue manager who once adjusted rates manually now interprets AI recommendations, validates them against market intelligence, and makes judgment calls that require contextual understanding.

This evolution demands new capabilities from hospitality professionals. AI literacy becomes foundational rather than optional. The ability to work alongside intelligent systems, to recognize their limitations, and to add value in the spaces between their capabilities defines the emerging hospitality professional.

What This Means for Hospitality Leadership & AI Adoption

Leadership responsibility is transforming in parallel with workforce evolution. The survey data points toward several dimensions of this transformation.

Brand relationships have become strategic assets in the AI era. Eighty-nine percent of hotel owners see working with a brand as beneficial when implementing AI. Thirty-four percent consider it essential, citing access to industry experts, pre-vetted technology, and available assistance. Fifty-five percent find it helpful for gaining a head start on AI adoption. Only 11% view brand relationships as limiting their AI technology choices.

This distribution reveals something important about the value proposition of brand affiliation in 2026. Brands that can deliver AI implementation guidance, reduce the learning curve, and de-risk technology investments have become more valuable to owners, rather than less valuable. The traditional brand value proposition of distribution and marketing now includes technology enablement and change management support.

Nearly all hotel owners (97%) indicate openness to joining or switching brands if the right opportunity presents itself. More than half (54%) describe themselves as mostly or completely open to such changes. This fluidity suggests that brand loyalty increasingly depends on demonstrated value in navigating industry transformation.

Loyalty programs have become more important in this environment. Sixty-five percent of owners consider a strong loyalty program of great importance to a hotel’s success, with 13% calling it critical and 52% calling it very important. The loyalty program has evolved from a marketing mechanism into an infrastructure for personalization that AI can enhance.

The financing environment appears to be easing. Sixty-one percent of owners find financing easier to access than a year ago. This improved capital availability creates opportunity for leaders who can articulate compelling AI investment cases and demonstrate clear pathways to returns.

Leadership in 2026 hospitality involves designing organizational capabilities rather than directing individual activities. The leader who can build AI literacy across their workforce, who can select and integrate the right technology partners, who can navigate the governance and security landscape, and who can maintain human connection while deploying automated systems will define competitive advantage.

Why Literacy and Visibility Matter

Two themes emerge from the survey data that deserve particular attention: the importance of organizational AI literacy and the strategic significance of visibility in AI-driven discovery systems.

The finding that 73% of owners want to expand AI but feel overwhelmed and uncertain about where to start represents a literacy gap. This gap exists at every level of hospitality organizations. Front-line staff need practical understanding of working alongside AI systems. Managers need capability to evaluate AI recommendations and make override decisions. Executives need strategic frameworks for AI investment and governance.

Addressing this literacy gap precedes effective AI scaling. Organizations that attempt to expand AI deployment without building foundational understanding across their workforce encounter the barriers the survey identifies: staff resistance, training challenges, and uncertainty about applications. Literacy comes before automation because automation without literacy produces confusion rather than capability.

Visibility has also taken on new meaning in the AI era. The survey shows hoteliers prioritizing marketing and sales improvements (20% of top capital investments) alongside technology investments (19%). What constitutes effective marketing and sales in 2026 differs fundamentally from previous eras.

Travelers increasingly discover hotels through AI-powered systems. Chatbots, voice assistants, and AI search engines make recommendations based on structured data, reviews, and content that these systems can parse and understand. A hotel’s visibility in these AI discovery systems has become as important as its visibility in traditional search engines.

The organizations that understand how AI systems surface recommendations, that structure their digital presence to be discoverable by these systems, and that maintain the data quality and content freshness that AI prioritizes will capture disproportionate attention in the booking funnel.

This visibility imperative connects directly to the technology investment priorities hoteliers identified. The properties that fail to optimize for AI discovery will find themselves increasingly invisible to the travelers who rely on AI-powered recommendations.

The Structural Shift in Hospitality

I spent years working in hospitality technology leadership. My time at Dolce Hotels and Resorts, which later became part of Wyndham Hotels and Resorts, gave me a front-row seat to industry transformation. I watched mobile adoption reshape guest expectations. I observed social media evolve from novelty to necessity. Each wave brought its own implementation challenges and its own organizational learning curve.

AI represents something different in scale and kind. Mobile and social media changed how guests interacted with hotels. AI is changing how hotels operate at their core. The 98% adoption figure reflects this difference. Previous technology waves showed gradual adoption curves over many years. AI adoption has compressed that timeline dramatically.

The hospitality industry has always been about human connection and memorable experiences. That fundamental purpose remains unchanged. What changes is how organizations create the conditions for those experiences. AI handles the predictable so humans can focus on the remarkable. It manages the routine so staff can attend to the exceptional. It optimizes the operational so leaders can concentrate on the strategic.

The 2026 Hotel Owner Trends Report captures an industry at a pivot point. The question is no longer whether to adopt AI. The challenge now involves building the organizational capability to deploy AI effectively, to scale it responsibly, and to maintain the human elements that define hospitality while leveraging the efficiency and personalization that AI enables.

Organizations that treat AI as a technology project will struggle. Those that recognize it as an organizational transformation will thrive.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What percentage of hotel owners have adopted AI in 2026?

Ninety-eight percent of hotel owners have incorporated AI into their businesses.

The Wyndham 2026 Hotel Owner Trends Report surveyed 325 hotel owners and property developers across the United States, Canada, and the Caribbean. Among respondents, 32% have incorporated AI into most aspects of their business, 42% have deployed AI in some areas with plans to expand, 23% have recently started, and only 2% have yet to begin. This near-universal adoption marks a significant inflection point for the hospitality industry.

2. What are the main barriers to expanding hotel AI adoption?

Data privacy concerns, investment costs, and legacy system integration top the list of barriers.

Survey respondents ranked data privacy and security concerns highest at 46%, followed by costs of investing in AI tools at 42%, and difficulty integrating AI with legacy systems at 40%. Training requirements (38%), staff resistance (38%), lack of understanding about AI applications (36%), infrastructure gaps (32%), and beliefs about guest preferences (29%) also contribute to expansion hesitation.

3. Where are hotels currently using AI most frequently?

Operational efficiency, energy efficiency, and revenue optimization lead current AI applications.

Sixty-four percent of hoteliers using AI deploy it for operational efficiency including AI-managed staffing, invoicing, and predictive maintenance. Energy efficiency applications such as automated lighting and HVAC adjustments follow at 54%. Revenue optimization through dynamic pricing and cost estimation ranks third at 53%. Guest-facing applications currently represent smaller portions of deployment.

4. How do hotel owners feel about AI making operational decisions?

Most are comfortable with AI decision-making, particularly with human oversight in place.

Forty percent of respondents expressed complete comfort with AI making operational decisions even without human oversight. Another 57% indicated comfort when human oversight is maintained. Only 3% reported discomfort even with oversight, and no respondents indicated complete discomfort. This suggests hospitality leaders view AI as a capable operational partner.

5. What is the industry outlook for hospitality in 2026?

Hotel owners express strong optimism about both short-term and long-term prospects.

Ninety percent of hotel owners have an optimistic outlook for 2026, with 42% extremely optimistic and 48% somewhat optimistic. The five-year outlook shows even greater confidence, with 94% expressing optimism. Seventy-nine percent of owners plan to expand their portfolios over the next five years, indicating strong growth orientation despite acknowledged challenges.

6. Why do hotel owners want to work with brands for AI implementation?

Brands provide expertise, pre-vetted technology, and implementation support that reduces risk.

Eighty-nine percent of hotel owners see brand relationships as beneficial for AI implementation. Thirty-four percent consider brands essential, citing access to industry experts, pre-vetted technology, and available assistance. Fifty-five percent find brands helpful for gaining a head start on AI adoption. Only 11% view brand requirements as limiting their technology choices.

7. What are the top investment priorities for hotels in 2026?

Staffing leads capital investment priorities, followed by marketing, property improvements, and technology.

Twenty-four percent of hotel owners will prioritize increased staffing for their greatest capital investments in 2026. Marketing and sales improvements and property improvements tie at 20% each. Technology investments follow at 19%, with amenity enhancements at 17%. The prioritization of staffing above technology suggests human capability remains central to competitive strategy.

8. How important are loyalty programs to hotel success?

Two-thirds of hotel owners consider strong loyalty programs essential or very important.

Sixty-five percent of owners believe a strong loyalty program is of great importance to hotel success. Thirteen percent rate loyalty programs as critical, while 52% consider them very important. Thirty-five percent view them as somewhat important. No respondents indicated that loyalty programs lack importance, reflecting their central role in the competitive landscape.

9. Why do 73% of hoteliers feel overwhelmed about AI expansion?

The gap between adoption and effective scaling requires organizational capabilities that many have yet to develop.

While 98% have adopted AI in some form, moving from initial deployment to comprehensive integration demands strategic clarity, workforce literacy, governance frameworks, and change management capability. The combination of rapid technology evolution, multiple potential applications, and organizational readiness gaps creates uncertainty about prioritization and implementation pathways.

10. What should hospitality leaders prioritize for AI success?

Building organizational AI literacy before expanding automation creates the foundation for sustainable scaling.

Organizations should invest in workforce capability development, establish clear governance frameworks, ensure infrastructure readiness, and develop strategic clarity about AI applications before pursuing aggressive expansion. This sequence addresses the primary barriers the survey identifies and positions organizations to scale AI effectively while maintaining the human connection that defines hospitality excellence.

Source: Wyndham Hotels & Resorts, “2026 Hotel Owner Trends Report,” conducted in partnership with Wakefield Research, November-December 2025. Survey of 325 hotel owners and property developers across the United States, Canada, and the Caribbean.
