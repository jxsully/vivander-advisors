AI Change Resilience Isn’t Just Strategy. It’s a Skillset.

Michael J. Goldrich · July 30, 2025


Why most AI strategies fail, what HBR got right, and how hotel teams can build muscle, not burnout

A recent article in Harvard Business Review, A Guide to Building Change Resilience in the Age of AI, by Karim R. Lakhani, Jen Stave, Douglas Ng, and Daniel Martines cuts to the heart of why most AI strategies stall. Despite the overwhelming consensus that AI will transform business, only 26% of companies report achieving measurable value. According to the BCG survey they cite, the biggest barriers aren’t technical. In fact, 70% are about people and process.

That statistic matches what I’ve seen firsthand in hospitality through our AI Mindset Program and the case studies outlined in The AI Literacy Playbook for Hoteliers. We’ve watched hotels adopt world-class AI tools but struggle to unlock real value because the teams weren’t equipped with the mindset or skillset to use them well. The missing piece? Change resilience.

In the book, I describe AI adoption not as a tool rollout but as a behavior shift. It’s not about knowing how to use ChatGPT. It’s about knowing when, why, and what to ask. That’s the literacy piece, and it’s where true transformation begins.

From Episodic Transformation to Continuous Rewiring

Lakhani and his co-authors make it clear: AI isn’t a one-time upgrade. It demands continuous adaptation. Legacy planning cycles and even agile practices can’t keep pace. Organizations need to develop what they call “change resilience,” which they define as the ability to sense, rewire, and lock in new ways of working.

This directly echoes our GEAR method in the Playbook: Gauge, Examine, Acquire, Reinforce. It is a structured way to move hotel teams from initial exposure to confident application. Each week builds on the last. We don’t just teach AI. We help people develop habits with it.

The book introduces what we call the 11-Minute by 11-Week Tipping Point. When someone saves just 11 minutes a day with AI for 11 weeks, the behavior becomes ingrained. It’s this kind of micro-shift, repeated across roles, that forms the real muscle of change resilience.

From Tool Fatigue to Targeted Interventions

The HBR article wisely advocates for “micro-bets” instead of massive transformations. Accenture, for example, encouraged every department to build small generative AI tools for real pain points. That’s the same philosophy we apply in our Week 2 and Week 4 training sessions.

In the Playbook, we outline multiple hospitality-specific use cases, broken down by role and AI exposure level. This allows teams to identify where to start based on their current workflow rather than a hypothetical end state.

We teach hoteliers to run 10-day experiments. A front desk associate might try generating guest amenity responses. A revenue analyst might summarize STR data. These aren’t proofs of concept for leadership. They are lived wins at the line level. That’s where momentum begins.

From Upskilling to Reimagining Roles

Lakhani and team stress that organizations must stop trying to modernize old operating models. Instead, they should reimagine roles around what AI can now do. Moderna merged its tech and HR functions to redefine responsibilities. Procter & Gamble created its own AI platform and tied upskilling to measurable business impact.

In the book, we describe a similar shift from roles to responsibilities. In Chapter 8: Building Custom GPTs, we show how hotels can offload SOPs, policies, and property knowledge into role-specific assistants. Suddenly, your sales manager isn’t just upskilled. They are supported by a trained AI sidekick that’s ready 24/7.

That’s what we mean when we say hotels must move beyond prompting and start thinking in terms of AI-enabled workflows. The task doesn’t vanish. The way it gets done changes.

From Fatigue to Flourishing

The final pillar of change resilience in the HBR article is care. They spotlight Cisco’s use of AI-powered wellbeing bots and team check-ins to prevent burnout. This couldn’t be more relevant to hospitality. The pace of change plus the intensity of guest service makes hotels especially vulnerable to digital fatigue.

In our Playbook, we describe a recurring theme across successful AI pilots. That theme is the presence of psychological safety. When teams feel permission to experiment without fear of judgment, learning accelerates. We help hotels create internal AI Champions. These are not technical experts, but trusted peers who normalize trial and error.

Our Week 6 wrap-up session is designed not just to evaluate success. It is a chance to create space for reflection, emotion, and support. It’s not enough to track productivity gains. You have to invest in the human response to disruption.

What’s Missing: System-Level Realities in Hospitality

What the HBR framework doesn’t fully address but what The AI Literacy Playbook for Hoteliers covers in depth is how fragmented and legacy-laden systems in hospitality slow everything down. AI doesn’t operate in a vacuum. It has to coexist with PMSs, CRMs, RMSs, and staffing constraints.

A hotel cannot rewire itself like Shopify if data lives in silos or if compliance blocks automation. That’s why our book dedicates Chapter 10 to Automation and Agents, and Chapter 11 to Tech Stack Transformation. It is not just about what people need to learn. It is about what systems need to support.

Final Takeaway

Karim R. Lakhani, Jen Stave, Douglas Ng, and Daniel Martines are right. AI transformation lives or dies on change resilience. But as we’ve seen in hotels, that resilience is built slowly at the ground level with confidence, clarity, and daily application.

The AI Literacy Playbook for Hoteliers doesn’t just teach tools. It builds teams that are ready to keep changing long after the pilot ends.

If your team doesn’t yet know what to ask, when to use AI, or how to measure the impact, start smaller. Start with the mindset. The rest will follow.
