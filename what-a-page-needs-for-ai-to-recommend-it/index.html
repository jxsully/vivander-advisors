What has to be on a page for AI to recommend it?

Michael J. Goldrich · September 2026

The answer to the customer's question, in the first sentence under a heading that asks it. Price stated, date recent, claims specific, facts in visible text. Vishwakarma and colleagues ran 252,000 trials across six AI models in 2026 with page length held constant and found four factors every model treated as a gate: a missing price, a low position in a list, an old timestamp, hedged language. Odds ratios above 100 on all six. I built the Vivander Advisors Answer Engine Content service on that finding and on Google's own extraction data. The checklist below is the one my audit scores.

AI does not navigate your site. It interrogates it.

The menu that was a PDF

A restaurant's tasting menu was a PDF. Beautiful. Downloadable. Invisible.

Its competitor's menu was HTML with prices on it. Every anniversary prompt on Perplexity recommended the competitor and cited the menu page.

The PDF became a page in one afternoon. Its 30-day re-test showed the venue cited for the first time. Nothing about the food changed.

Why the answer has to come first

Assistants lift fragments, not pages. DEJAN read Google's grounding output across 883,262 snippets and found extraction at roughly fifteen-word chunks with a heavy lead bias. Opening paragraphs lifted almost whole. Grounding plateauing near 540 words no matter how long the page ran. Survival falling from 61 percent for pages under a thousand words to 13 percent for pages over three thousand.

Answer-first is one of only two levers a 45-study review grades "strong."

And there is no word count for the opening answer. The "40 to 60 words" figure that circulates came from featured-snippet research, a search feature, not an AI-citation finding. Score the ordering. Never the count.

What facts have to be visible?

For a hotel: the rate or rate range, room count, location relative to landmarks, amenities by name, parking, pet and accessibility policies, check-in times. And for a restaurant: the menu in HTML, prices, hours, private dining capacity and minimums, the reservation path.

If it is not in the initial HTML as text, no engine that fetches the page directly can use it. searchVIU placed a price only in JSON-LD markup in December 2025 and found zero of five AI systems extracted it on direct fetch. Only visible HTML was read.

Omitting price is the failure I see most in hospitality. It is also the one every model in the 252,000-trial study punished hardest. Twenty years of "we don't compete on price" trained an industry to hide the one fact the engine needs first.

Does the heading have to ask the question?

Yes. The heading is the addressable unit. Body text that answers a question while no heading asks it does not count as coverage in my content architecture score, and question coverage carries 40 percent of that score. Microsoft's own guidance says assistants can often lift question-and-answer pairs word for word. A page with fewer than five headings caps how many passages it can offer at all.

The most valuable rows in any audit I run are the ones I mark implicit. A fact is on the page. No heading asks the question. Extraction happens by luck. Those rows need an editor, not a budget.

Do sentences have to stand alone?

Yes. Extraction happens at sentence granularity. A sentence that opens on "it" or "this" cannot be lifted, because the reader of the fragment has no idea what "it" was. Name the subject. "The Beachfront Suite sleeps four and starts at $540 in summer" travels. "It sleeps four and starts at $540" does not.

Does hidden content count?

No. Microsoft's guidance: do not hide important answers in tabs or expandable menus. Non-rendering crawlers read the initial HTML. A rate behind a tab, a menu in a PDF, a policy in an accordion, a capacity behind "inquire": none of them exist to the retrieval step.

Does the page need a named person?

It should. Google states that AI-surface clicks favor content that is richer and deeper from someone who brought their expertise, and that repetitive content joins spam in downranking whether a person or a model wrote it. The GM on why the policy exists. Or the chef on why the menu changed. That is the paragraph a competitor cannot copy, and I do not ship a page without one.

Three things worth sitting with

For your business. Put the question in the heading, the answer in the first sentence, the price in the text. Then cut to density. A tight 800-word page gets over half its content grounded. One of 4,000 words gets an eighth.

For your role. Content teams now write for machines and humans at the same time. That is a new skill, and most of the people who have it learned it on the job this year.

For your guests. They asked a specific question. Your page answered a general one, beautifully, in paragraph six. The assistant never got that far.

What I do not know

Whether the four gatekeepers transfer from product reviews to hotel and restaurant pages at the same strength. The direction matches everything else I measure. Its magnitude in hospitality is untested, and my own re-tests are the closest measurement anyone has.

Where this is going

I told hotels a year ago that rich schema was the weapon. The evidence moved. A controlled study found no citation lift from markup, and the extraction data says the engines read the visible page. So I moved with the evidence, and I say so, because the alternative is defending advice the data retired.

Your site can no longer be a slideshow of pretty pictures. It has to function like a knowledge engine, and a knowledge engine states things.

The assistant is not reading your brand story. It is reading your rates page, and there was no rate on it.
