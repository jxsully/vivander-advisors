What is the Generative AI Productivity Paradox?

Michael J. Goldrich · September 2026

The Generative AI Productivity Paradox is the pattern in which generative AI tools cut the time a task takes and total work goes up anyway, because people take on more tasks, broaden their roles and extend their hours voluntarily. I named it in my presentation at the HFTP CIO Summit in September 2024. An eight-month Harvard Business Review study of 200 employees later documented the same pattern: people with AI worked faster, took on broader work and extended their hours, and nobody assigned the extra work. The paradox is the reason AI pilots that "save time" so often show no result a CFO can find.

AI tools promised less work. They delivered more. Voluntarily.

Why does it happen?

Three mechanisms I keep seeing in hospitality teams.

The saved time has no owner, so it is absorbed by the next task in the queue. Tools make previously impossible work feel possible, so the scope of a role expands to fill the hour. And nobody agreed in advance what result would count as proof, so a faster team and a busier team look identical on the org chart.

Why does it matter for hotel companies?

Because the industry moved past "should we" faster than anyone predicted. Wyndham's research found 98 percent of hotel owners had adopted AI somewhere in their operation and 73 percent felt overwhelmed about what comes next. That gap tells the whole story.

Deloitte spent two years interviewing over 1,000 industry professionals and concluded that the employee who learns to orchestrate AI agents becomes the most valuable person in the building. If the hour the tool gives back is never assigned, the orchestration never happens and the pilot never scales.

What did the hospitality industry foundation research add?

The research series I authored for the hospitality industry foundation asked commercial leaders one question. If this technology reduces an eight-hour workday to seven, how do you want associates to spend the extra hour?

Most had never been asked. Some had never considered it. The answer decides whether the investment was worth it, and it belongs in the business case before the tool is bought.

What is the fix?

Name the person who owns whether the AI initiative worked. Agree what proof looks like before the pilot starts. Count routines adopted per department, hours saved as reported by the people doing the work, and the usage policy in place. Decide what the recovered hour is for: guest contact, revenue work, training, or leaving on time.

Review it at 30 and 90 days. My AI literacy programs end by naming that owner. And my advisory sprints start there.

Where this is going

Fifty-eight percent of enterprise IT decision-makers are piloting AI agents, per Wharton and GBK Collective. Note the word. Piloting. Not deploying, not generating returns. The stuck zone.

The relationship is inverting. Soon the AI does the work and the human assists the AI. It sounds like a small reordering of words. In practice it rewires the job, the tooling and what an hour is for.

The paradox is not a bug in the tools. It is the absence of a decision. Make the decision and the paradox goes away.
