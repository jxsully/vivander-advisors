A couple staying near a city waterfront opens an AI assistant late in the afternoon and asks where to eat dinner tonight. They want seafood, a view of the water, and a table they can still get. The answer names a handful of restaurants and puts one forward. They book from the link in the answer and never see a page of search results.The restaurant at the center of this paper sits a short walk from that couple. It holds a national wine magazine’s award, its city magazine’s best-of title for 2026, a 4.7 rating on its reservation platform, and a sibling restaurant in another market with a loyal following. Across fifty-five questions written the way diners ask, AI tools mentioned it in about a quarter of the answers. They recommended it in about one in twenty.Nobody at the restaurant could have known that. The reservation book records the guests who arrived. It has no column for the couple sent somewhere else.Everything that follows comes from a real Vivander audit, collected in the summer of 2026. The restaurant, its city, and its competitors are not named, and review counts are rounded. Every other figure comes directly from the audit’s saved answers.

Why should a restaurant assess AI visibility?

The proposal for a restaurant matches the proposal for any hospitality business. Commission a baseline audit. Ask the questions diners ask, on the six AI platforms they use, three times each. Check every claim the platforms make against the facts. Turn the findings into a ranked list of fixes with written targets, then re-test.This audit produced 1,386 saved answers to 77 questions. Fifty-five never mentioned the restaurant. Twenty-two asked about it by name.

What is the gap between being known and being recommended?

Pew Research Center found in February 2026 that 42 percent of U.S. adults use AI chatbots to search for information.[1] Choosing a restaurant is among the most ordinary searches there is, and the answer an AI tool gives is short. A few names appear, and most people act on what is in front of them.Exhibit 1

Real audit result, anonymized. A score of 51 falls in the Structured band (40 to 59).AI can reach the site, and the restaurant’s reputation is strong. Its own pages are the weak point. Ten pages were scored on whether they answer diners’ questions with evidence, and they averaged 4.2 out of 30. Dish names and prices sat inside image descriptions. Menus lived in PDFs behind collapsed sections. Policies diners ask about, from dress code to service fee to children, appeared nowhere.One finding captures the problem in a single image. The website runs on a commerce platform that automatically labeled the restaurant’s merchandise store for machines, with full product data for seventeen items. For the restaurant itself, the code carried no machine-readable label at all: no address, no hours, no menu, no reservation link. Errors drawn from the 22 questions that named the restaurant; 14 branded answers were wrong or stale at baseline.The audit found conflicting hours and capacity statements on the restaurant’s own website, alongside stale coverage and other possible contributors. Platforms contradicted one another on five of ten fact types. Of 43 citations to the restaurant’s site, 18 pointed to previous website addresses, including one that returned an error. The audited restaurant appeared on every one of these questions and was picked three times across all seven.That shared address carries the sharpest example in the audit. The restaurant occupies a site once home to one of the city’s best-known restaurants, a name many locals still attach to the pier. Asked what restaurants operate there now, five of six platforms named the audited restaurant, almost always in second place. All six named Competitor A first. Nothing on the restaurant’s website tells the story of the site it occupies.Reviews round out the picture. Review platforms supplied 54 percent of the sources the audit could classify, and the reservation platform was the single most cited source in the entire test, with 811 citations. The six newest Google reviews had no replies, and the restaurant held 17 reviews on a major travel review site where its leading competitors held thousands across platforms. Booking from an AI answer worked well through the reservation platform (85 of 100) and through Google (90). Through the restaurant’s own site the path scored 45, because the booking link sat inside a collapsed section and the booking page carried no text of its own.

How will monitoring track what changes?

Baseline and planned checks, not completed improvement results. Re-tests had not run when the source case was prepared. Repeated answers can show change; they cannot on their own establish which correction caused it.
Monitoring repeats the agreed questions and compares new evidence with the baseline. It can reveal whether errors persist and whether mentions or recommendations change. Platforms, competing sources and other conditions also change, so a before-and-after result alone cannot establish causation.Exhibit 7

At thirty days, the read grades the first three sprints. The ninety-day read grades all five and compares every pair with the baseline answers. One diner question was deliberately left out of every fix, so it can serve as a control: if that answer moves too, something other than the restaurant’s work is moving the platforms.

What are the limits of this audit?

Read the findings within their test conditions. These are observations of platform answers, not evidence of lost or recovered sales, guest behavior or a guaranteed recommendation.
Answers were what the audit counted. Covers were not, and analytics access was not part of the engagement, so AI referral traffic went unmeasured.An outside reviewer checked 63 statements in the delivered documents. Twenty-nine were confirmed outright, seven partly confirmed, and seven found incorrect. No score changed. The incorrect statements were corrected, and every document carries dated notes saying what changed and why. A client should expect a clear record of corrections and unresolved issues.

How should your organization start?

Choose the questions that carry the most revenue: the private dining inquiry, the special-occasion dinner, the visitor deciding tonight. Agree on the competitive set and on who confirms the facts. Fix the facts first, because AI is repeating the website’s own contradictions today. Then re-test, and let the answers decide what comes next.Restaurants have always lived on word of mouth. A regular tells a colleague. Visitors ask the concierge. The newest voice in that conversation is a machine, and it repeats whatever the restaurant’s own pages tell it, contradictions included.

About the audit evidence

Exhibits 1 through 7 report results from a real Vivander Visibility Audit of a full-service restaurant. Answers were collected in the summer of 2026 from ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Gemini, Google AI Overviews, and Google AI Mode. The restaurant, its location, its competitors, and identifying details are withheld, and review counts are rounded.The audit asked 77 questions: 55 that named no restaurant, written in five diner voices, and 22 that named the restaurant to test what AI knows. Each question ran three times on each platform from a clean session, and a result counted when it appeared in at least two of three runs. Pairs where a platform returned no AI answer were excluded from every count.Audit results describe what the tested platforms said on the test date. They do not measure how many diners saw those answers, estimate lost revenue, or predict future recommendations.
