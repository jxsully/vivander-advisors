Give AI More to Work With: Build Credible Evidence Beyond the Website

Michael J. Goldrich · October 17, 2026

A restaurant describes its private dining room as “perfect for every occasion.” A local dining publication explains that the room seats 18, has a separate entrance and works well for business dinners.

The second description gives a customer something to evaluate. It also gives a search-enabled assistant a more specific source to consult if that page is retrieved.

This is a useful starting point for third-party signal optimization: build public evidence that answers the questions a buyer actually asks.

The aim is not a particular number of mentions. Ten vague promotional references may tell a customer less than one detailed, relevant account. Nor is every third-party page independent: a paid placement or syndicated press release needs to be understood for what it is.

Begin with the decision, then look for evidence

“How can the business get more coverage?” is a broad brief. “What evidence would help a buyer evaluate this service for this particular need?” leads to more useful work.

A hotel courting small executive retreats might need public detail about meeting space, group arrangements and transport. A consultancy offering AI training might need evidence about the audience, exercises and deliverables. A software business might need a credible account of how an integration works.

Map a few important buying questions to the evidence available today. Identify where the business has a substantiated answer but little public documentation. Also identify where the answer is still uncertain. Coverage cannot repair a capability the business does not have.

Understand what platform guidance does establish

Google says local results consider relevance, distance and prominence, and discusses links and reviews in that context. These are documented considerations for Google local ranking. They are not a published formula for every AI recommendation.

Google’s guidance on helpful content also encourages original information, clear sourcing and evidence of experience. That is a sound editorial direction for evidence a business publishes or contributes to.

The practical inference is to make relevant, credible information available where customers look. Neither document establishes how much weight an individual AI answer will assign to a particular review or publication.

Ask for honest reviews that describe a real experience

A useful review tells readers what happened. A customer may explain the problem, the service used, the experience and any limitations. The detail helps a future buyer judge fit.

Ask customers for honest feedback after a real interaction. Keep the request neutral. Avoid scripts telling them which claims to make or language to repeat for AI visibility.

For Google reviews, follow the platform’s rules: Google prohibits incentives in exchange for reviews. Check the applicable rules before using any review platform.

Read the feedback as operational information, too. If several customers describe a confusing booking process, improving that process may be a more valuable intervention than requesting more reviews.

Give publishers something worth covering

A useful pitch starts with information an editor’s audience can use. That might be a well-documented project, a practical method, an original study or a substantive change in service.

Prepare the evidence before approaching a publisher. Explain what was measured, when, how the work was performed and what the findings cannot support. Obtain any necessary permission before sharing a client’s information.

A hypothetical training case illustrates the difference. “AI workshop transforms productivity” makes a large claim. A case explaining the tasks practiced, evaluation method and observed results gives readers a way to assess the work. If productivity was not measured, the case should not claim a productivity improvement.

A publisher may independently examine the evidence, ask difficult questions or decline the story. That editorial judgment is part of what distinguishes independent coverage from a business repeating its own message.

Choose profiles and directories for relevance

Start with sources buyers already use in the category: relevant professional associations, tourism organizations, review platforms or industry directories. Confirm eligibility and keep the description accurate.

Look at the quality of the information, the audience and the maintenance process. A directory that publishes stale entries and accepts any claim offers a different kind of evidence from an organization that verifies credentials.

When AI answers cite particular sources repeatedly, investigate them. Their appearance provides a research lead. It does not prove that buying a listing there will produce a recommendation.

Measure the content of a mention

A useful mention names the right business, describes a relevant capability and gives enough context for a reader to understand the claim. Record whether the source is independent, sponsored or syndicated.

Then inspect sampled AI answers separately. Did they name the business? Present it as a suitable choice? Cite the business website or an external source? Those observations answer different questions.

A publication can earn a citation while the business receives the recommendation. A business can also be named without a citation. A website-only citation count misses part of that picture.

For the next month, choose one important buyer question and one genuine evidence gap. Improve the underlying evidence, make it available through an appropriate source and record what changes. That gives the team a concrete piece of work to review.
