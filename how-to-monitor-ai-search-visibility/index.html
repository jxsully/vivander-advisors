The short answer: choose a stable set of buyer questions, run them under documented conditions, save the full responses and compare separate measures over time. Use the findings to decide what to improve.

An AI answer can change with wording, model, account settings, location, available sources and timing. A screenshot of one favorable answer is useful evidence of that run. It does not prove that every buyer sees the same result.

Keep the measures distinct

Four questions for every answer

01

Was your organization mentioned?

Record whether the brand appears and the context. Separate a passing mention from a response to a buyer’s actual need.

02

Was your organization cited or recommended?

Save linked sources. Record explicit recommendations separately from mentions and citations.

03

Was the description accurate?

Check services, availability, geography, prices and contact details against current, authoritative facts.

04 Did it lead to a useful outcome? Review available referral traffic, qualified inquiries and customer feedback. Attribution can be incomplete; do not treat visibility as proof of revenue.

Build a repeatable baseline.

  1. Choose the audience and questions. Include discovery, comparison and purchase-intent questions. Keep branded questions separate from questions that do not name your organizationr organization.
  2. Select the tools and conditions. Record the tool, model or mode when shown, search setting, date and relevant account or location context. Use a fresh conversation for comparable runs.
  3. Save the whole answer. Keep the question, complete response and linked sources. Record no-answer results and errors rather than silently dropping them.
  4. Repeat important questions. Use more than one run when a decision depends on the result. Record variability instead of presenting a single answer as universal.
  5. Apply a consistent rubric. Use written definitions for mention, citation, recommendation and factual error. Have a person review ambiguous cases.
  6. Set the cadence and owner. Start with a practical schedule, such as monthly, and add checks after significant business or website changes. Assign someone to review the findings.

Turn changes into decisions.

If an answer misstates your offer, check whether your own pages are clear and current. Inspect the cited sources before deciding where to correct the information.

If a competitor appears, compare the evidence used to support that recommendation. Look for a useful information gap your organization can address, rather than copying the competitor’s wording.

If your visibility changes suddenly, first check for changes in the question set, model or measurement method. Record website updates in a change log so your organization can investigate possible explanations without claiming causation.

For Google AI search features, review the platform’s own reporting guidance. Google explains eligibility and measurement for AI features. Your cross-platform monitoring record answers a separate question: what selected tools returned for your defined tests.

Questions people ask

How often should your organization check AI visibility?

Choose a cadence that supports a decision. Monthly can be a useful starting point, with additional checks after important changes. More frequent monitoring needs a clear purpose and enough review capacity.

Can your organization monitor AI visibility manually?

Yes. A spreadsheet can support an initial question set. As scope grows, consider tooling for collection and comparison while retaining human review of evidence and factual errors.

Is one overall visibility score enough?

Use separate measures and show the sample behind them. An overall score can hide inaccurate descriptions, weak citations or changes in the question mix.
