Who Owns the Guest in the Age of AI? A Closer Look at Booking.com’s Global AI Sentiment Report

Michael J. Goldrich · July 30, 2025

The OTA’s AI-powered vision of the future may seem seamless, but it risks sidelining hotels in their own guest journey.

Booking.com’s Global AI Sentiment Report 2025 is a global, data-driven look at how consumers perceive artificial intelligence. Drawing on over 37,000 respondents across 33 countries, the report feels comprehensive and well-timed. It captures growing familiarity with AI, a cautiously optimistic outlook among travelers, and rapid adoption across every stage of the trip. The message is clear: AI is here, and it is shaping the future of travel.

But beneath the data lies a story with higher stakes. This is a positioning document. Booking.com frames itself not simply as a travel platform, but as the new operating system for travel. AI becomes the medium, and Booking.com becomes the messenger. Hotels are cast in a supporting role, optimized into invisibility.

For hoteliers focused on owning the guest relationship and growing direct bookings, this report is a strategic warning.

The Promise of AI in Travel: More Personal, More Predictable, More Platform-Driven

The report highlights several ways travelers are embracing AI.

  • 67 percent of global travelers have used AI during a trip.
  • 62 percent have used it to plan or book.
  • 66 percent say AI makes travel easier and more efficient.
  • AI assistants are more trusted than influencers and even work colleagues.

Travelers want speed, convenience, and personalized help. AI is delivering that, often better than traditional search or human recommendations. Booking.com points to its own tools like the AI Trip Planner, Smart Filters, and Instant Answer Assistants, as examples of this evolution.

What is missing from this vision is the hotel itself. AI is framed as something that platforms deploy on behalf of travelers, automating the journey and managing the experience before the guest ever reaches the front desk.

The Silent Rewrite of the Customer Journey

This report subtly reorders the customer journey in a way that centers the OTA. The traveler begins with a question. That question is answered by an agent or assistant controlled by the platform. The platform narrows the options, customizes recommendations, and facilitates the booking. The hotel appears only at the end, almost like a product that has been selected and shipped.

Every early touchpoint: intent, planning, filtering, booking is owned by Booking.com.

And this is where the power shift becomes visible. The hotel is a line item. It loses visibility, voice, and influence in the very moments that matter most.

The Guest Relationship at Risk

For years, hoteliers have invested in tools to reduce reliance on OTAs. They have built direct booking campaigns, improved user experience on hotel websites, and focused on loyalty. They have worked hard to move up the funnel, connecting with guests earlier in the journey. AI, as described in this report, unravels that work.

If Booking.com becomes the conversational layer between the traveler and the world, then hotels risk becoming invisible within that exchange. The guest may never visit the hotel’s website. They may never compare offers outside of the OTA interface. They may never know that the hotel had a better deal or a unique experience waiting for them.

This is not just a marketing problem. It is a strategic one. It is a question of control. Who owns the narrative? Who holds the relationship? Who gets the data, the loyalty, and the next booking?

The Platform’s Advantage and Its Agenda

Throughout the report, Booking.com frames itself as both pioneer and protector. It is described as a leader in AI, a builder of trust, and a champion of ethical deployment. The implication is subtle but powerful: AI is too big, too complex, and too risky for individual hotels to manage. Better to trust the platform.

And yet, this framing serves a purpose. If AI is always positioned as a platform-led innovation, then the OTA becomes indispensable. The report never once mentions hotel brands building their own AI tools. It never references first-party data. It never asks how hotels might personalize their experiences or retain control of the guest journey using their own AI capabilities.

This is not an oversight. It is a business strategy.

Regional Trends Reinforce the Message

The report points out that travelers in APAC and LATAM are far more comfortable using AI throughout their trips. They use it to discover destinations, translate in real time, and get travel updates. These markets are described as AI-forward, eager, and experimental.

But again, the benefits of that openness are funneled toward the platform. There is no call to action for local hotels to build language tools or destination guides. The message is consistent: innovation should come from above, not be built from within.

Trust and the Language of Stewardship

Trust is a recurring theme in the report. Only 6 percent of consumers fully trust AI outputs. Many fact-check the information they receive. Most want a human in the loop.

Booking.com presents itself as a responsible actor, one that will balance innovation with transparency. It talks about privacy, bias, and the importance of ethics.

But it never once suggests that hotels could do the same. It does not explore what trust looks like in a first-party context. It does not ask what transparency means when a guest books directly and receives follow-up from the GM or front office. The report assumes that stewardship belongs to the platform.

That assumption is a risk. If hoteliers internalize it, they will cede the future of the guest relationship by default.

Five Critical Blind Spots in the Report

  1. Direct channel innovation is ignored. There is no acknowledgment that hotels might deploy their own AI tools to compete on speed, personalization, or accuracy.
  2. First-party data is left out. Hotels sit on powerful behavioral and transactional data, yet the report does not explore how this data can be activated for smarter experiences.
  3. Prompt optimization and AI visibility are never mentioned. The new discovery layer is built on generative search and AI assistants, but hotels are not given a role in optimizing for it.
  4. No mention of AI partnerships or open tools. With platforms like ChatGPT Teams and Claude, hotels can now build AI that reflects their brand and voice, but the report is silent on these developments.
  5. The guest’s emotional journey is flattened. AI is framed as efficient and scalable, but not necessarily memorable or human. Hotels know that great service lives in nuance. That nuance is absent here.

This Is a Playbook.

Booking.com has created an impressive, well-researched artifact that showcases the future of AI in travel. But it also functions as a roadmap for platform dominance. It promotes a future where the guest never leaves the walled garden. It celebrates convenience but obscures control. It implies safety in scale while downplaying the creativity and authenticity that live within hotels themselves.

The question facing hoteliers is not whether AI will shape the guest journey. It already is. The question is whether they will participate in that shaping or be shaped by someone else’s system.

Ownership of the guest relationship does not disappear overnight. It erodes through inaction. It slips away when platforms do the thinking, the filtering, and the engaging. AI does not just change the tools. It changes the terms.

And for those in hospitality, the time to renegotiate those terms is now.
