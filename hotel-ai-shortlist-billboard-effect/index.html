What the billboard effect teaches hospitality about AI visibility and reservations

By Michael J. Goldrich · October 16, 2026 · 10 minute read

The commercial takeaway

AI can introduce a hotel or restaurant before the guest visits its website or makes a reservation. Test whether the business appears, whether the recommendation is accurate and whether the guest can verify and book the promised experience. Visibility monitoring and referral analytics answer different questions; neither alone proves incremental bookings.

Drive past a billboard for a hotel or restaurant. You notice the name. Later, when you need somewhere to stay or eat, that name feels familiar. You search for it and make a reservation.

Did the billboard work? In our gut, we understand how it could. Showing exactly which reservation it caused is harder.

Outdoor advertising has developed sophisticated ways to measure exposure. Geopath combines traffic and travel data, mobile-device panels and visibility research to estimate how many people are likely to see an advertisement and how often.[1] Counting cars is only the beginning. The sign’s position, the direction of travel and the opportunity to notice it matter too.

Those estimates help advertisers make decisions. Connecting exposure to a specific person’s later purchase is a different measurement problem. A useful estimate of audience does not automatically establish why someone bought.

Hospitality has encountered this problem before. Chris Anderson gave us a way to examine it.

When the billboard became a hotel listing

For his 2009 Cornell report, Anderson worked with four JHM hotels and Expedia. He cycled the properties on and off Expedia for seven to eleven days at a time. Each spent forty days displayed at the top of the first page and forty days absent. He then compared reservations through the hotels’ own channels during those periods, excluding Expedia bookings.[2]

The study estimated a 7.5 to 26 percent increase in own-channel reservations when the hotels appeared on Expedia. That included their websites and other channels they controlled, on top of the reservations Expedia processed.

The proposed explanation was commercially important: a traveler could discover a hotel on Expedia, learn enough to consider it and then book elsewhere. The place that introduced the hotel did not necessarily receive the reservation.

This was a small, four-property pseudo-experiment, not a universal uplift every hotel could apply to its budget. A 2017 follow-up by Anderson and Saram Han found that the billboard effect persisted in travelers’ research behavior, although its influence might have weakened. It was not a repeat of the original listing-and-removal experiment.[2]

The lesson I take from this is that discovery and transaction can happen in different places. Measuring only the final booking route can miss an earlier influence.

The question I asked Chris

As Chris and I walked from a conference, I asked him about the new billboard effect in AI. How do you know when an AI recommendation influenced a booking? How do you attribute it? How do you measure it?

Those questions bring me back to the roadside billboard. We cannot see every private AI conversation that might lead to a reservation. But we can systematically inspect how AI answers a defined set of relevant customer questions.

My view is that, just as outdoor measurement samples and models traffic, we need to sample prompts. We need a disciplined way to observe whether a business appears, what AI says about it and where the customer can go next. That begins with a fundamental diagnostic of the business’s visibility foundation, followed by ongoing monitoring.

Start with the foundation

Every business needs to understand whether its digital foundation supports being found and accurately represented. An attractive homepage and a favorable answer to one prompt are insufficient evidence.

A useful diagnostic checks whether relevant pages can be crawled and their important content read. It checks whether the business’s name, location and identity are consistent across its website, listings and reservation partners. Are hours, amenities, menus, fees and policies accurate? Do reviews raise recurring issues that the business needs to address? Can a customer distinguish one property or location from another?

Then test the information against real customer intent. Can the late-arriving traveler establish how check-in works? Can a diner find a suitable option for ten people? Are restrictions and conditions clear? Does the booking link lead to the correct location and preserve the details needed to act?

The output should be a prioritized set of repairs with named owners. Fix an unreadable menu, conflicting hours or a broken reservation route before celebrating a visibility score.

SEO remains part of the foundation. Google’s guidance retains established search fundamentals for its AI features; special AI markup is neither required nor a guarantee of inclusion.[3] The diagnostic identifies weaknesses and establishes a baseline. It cannot certify permanent visibility.

Sample the prompts and keep watching

A roadside billboard stays in a known location. An AI answer can change with the wording, platform, context and timing of the question. One successful screenshot tells us very little about consistency.

Build a bounded set of prompts around the customers and situations that matter to the business: a quiet hotel after a late flight, dinner for ten near a theater, a private room for a celebration. Use unbranded questions to examine discovery. Asking about your business by name tests a different question.

Keep the core wording and constraints stable. As a practical starting routine, run each prompt three times on each relevant AI platform in fresh conversations. Record the date, platform, available model or mode, location settings and account context. Save the answers and their cited sources. Repeat on a regular schedule and after important information changes.

Three runs can expose variation. They do not make a representative sample of customer demand or prove an answer is stable. Without a defensible sampling frame and weighting, report results as observations within your selected prompt set, not market-wide visibility or reach.

Track citations, mentions and recommendations separately. Then check whether the answer is accurate, respects the customer’s constraints and offers a working route to the intended action. A recommendation for the wrong location or an unavailable service deserves attention even if it looks like a visibility win.

This is why I see a role for ongoing monitoring solutions like what we have built at Vivander. The diagnostic establishes the starting point; monitoring should show directional changes in visibility, accuracy, recommendation consistency and the routes offered to customers. Use those observations to investigate, correct and retest. They do not, by themselves, count bookings caused by AI.

Give the guest something to verify

The commercial question begins once the business appears. Put the AI recommendation beside your reservation page. Could a guest book the experience the AI just described?

Consider a hotel recommended for a quiet stay after a late flight. Its website celebrates the rooftop bar but says little about room location, overnight arrival or mandatory fees. A restaurant makes a shortlist for a business dinner, but its site has an old menu and no clear answer about a table for ten.

Both businesses earned attention. Both now make the customer work to establish whether that attention was deserved.

Markus Busch explores this challenge as the Shortlist Effect in Hospitality.today: AI can introduce a business while the customer verifies it and completes the transaction somewhere else.[4] Recommendation, verification and action may leave different digital footprints.

Yext’s 2026 research illustrates the verification habit. Among surveyed AI users, 49 percent said they visited a business website after an AI recommendation. This was general local-business research with multiple responses allowed, not a measure of hotel-booking conversion.[5]

For the hotel, explain overnight check-in, disclose fees and describe which room categories may suit a quieter stay. Distinguish a request from a confirmed allocation. For the restaurant, publish a current, readable menu, accurate hours, group-size guidance and a clear reservation route. Explain how allergy requests are handled and how to contact the team; an AI answer must never become a safety guarantee.

Keep the facts consistent across the site, profiles and partners. Marketing, reservations and operations need to agree on what the business can actually deliver.

Make the direct choice worth making

Once a guest has verified the fit, the direct experience needs a reason to win. Flexible terms, a useful package, a specific room choice or responsive help may matter alongside price.

Cost the offer before promoting it. Breakfast and late checkout carry food, labor, housekeeping and inventory costs. A restaurant’s group package needs the same discipline around service time, food cost and table capacity.

Compare contribution after acquisition, payment, booking and fulfillment costs. Avoiding a commission creates potential room to invest; the entire commission is not automatically available to spend. Direct reservations still cost money, and a perk may go to someone who would have booked anyway.

Measure conversion, cancellations, service consequences and margin together. The aim is a better commercial outcome that delivers something the guest values.

Build a measurement picture you can defend

There is a measurable AI referral channel. Adobe reported that AI-referred traffic to US travel sites grew 119 percent year over year in July 2026.[6] That measures tracked visits from AI links. It does not count every AI-influenced guest or establish incremental revenue.

Review referrals alongside branded search, guest feedback and prompt monitoring. Each answers a different question. A guest may discover a hotel in an assistant, search its name later and book directly. Analytics can accurately record that arrival while leaving the earlier influence unseen.

An optional discovery question can capture guests’ recollections, although memory and response bias limit the conclusions. Keep it brief and avoid collecting private AI conversations.

Use platform reporting where available. Google has introduced dedicated generative-AI performance reporting in Search Console.[7] It adds visibility into Google’s own experiences without reconstructing a customer’s separate conversations across assistants and devices.

Tagged links can help preserve a recorded source. Cross-domain measurement may connect a website to its booking engine when supported and properly configured, with necessary permissions and consent. Widgets, redirects, app handoffs and privacy choices can still leave gaps.[8]

For a small hotel or restaurant, a handful of bookings can make percentages swing sharply. Review actual counts and longer patterns. If bookings rise after a website repair, check rates, availability, seasonality, events, paid media and reviews before crediting AI. A properly designed test or credible causal analysis is needed to claim incremental lift. Prompt monitoring alone cannot supply that evidence.

Own the whole promise

Some journeys already let customers move from AI discovery toward restaurant reservations inside an assistant or through a provider.[9] Personal agents may reduce website visits further. Adoption and operating limits will vary; there is no credible deadline at which hospitality websites become unnecessary.

The responsibility remains: accurate information must reach whoever is deciding, availability and terms must survive the handoff, and a capable person must be reachable when the plan becomes complicated.

The roadside billboard taught us to take exposure seriously even when the path to purchase is difficult to trace. Anderson’s work showed why hospitality should examine the influence of a channel beyond the reservations it processes.

For AI, start with the diagnostic. Sample the prompts. Keep monitoring. Then follow the guest all the way to the reservation.

If we earned a place on the shortlist today, have we made it easy to choose us with confidence?

Common questions about AI visibility and reservations

What is the AI shortlist effect in hospitality?

It describes a journey in which AI introduces a hotel or restaurant, then the guest verifies the recommendation and may reserve elsewhere. Markus Busch develops this idea as the Shortlist Effect. It is a useful way to examine discovery and conversion, not proof of a universal booking uplift.

Can AI visibility monitoring prove that AI caused a booking?

No. Monitoring observes answers within a defined set of prompts. Referral analytics records identifiable visits. Neither reveals every private AI conversation or establishes incremental bookings. A credible causal analysis or a properly designed experiment is needed to claim incremental lift.

What should a hotel or restaurant fix first?

Start with accurate, readable facts and a working reservation route: room details or menus, hours, location, arrival arrangements, fees and policies. Then test unbranded guest questions repeatedly, preserve the answers and sources, and assign owners to the errors that affect a guest’s decision.
