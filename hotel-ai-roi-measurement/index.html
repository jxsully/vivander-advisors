We Bought AI. What Actually Changed at the Hotel?

Michael J. Goldrich · October 29, 2026

The invoice is easy to find.

The result sometimes requires a search party.

A hotel buys an AI tool. The rollout happens. People complete training. Usage rises. At the next ownership meeting, someone asks what changed in the operation.

The answer should be more useful than a count of logins.

Hotels measure AI ROI by comparing a defined workflow’s business results with its full implementation and operating costs. Track quality alongside speed, count the human review and rework, and distinguish newly available staff capacity from expenses that actually disappeared.

The most useful starting question is small enough to answer: which piece of work should improve, for whom, and by how much?

What should an AI pilot measure?

Choose a workflow with a visible beginning, a clear completion point and a responsible owner.

“Improve guest communications” leaves too much room for interpretation. “Reduce the staff time needed to resolve routine pre-arrival parking questions, without increasing incorrect answers or repeat contacts” gives the team something to test.

For that workflow, track:

  • Eligible inquiries received and the share actually using AI assistance.
  • Staff handling time, including checking, corrections and escalation.
  • Whether the guest’s question was resolved.
  • Repeat contacts, material errors and service recovery.
  • Any measurable effect on costs or contribution.

Agree on what counts as resolved. A fast answer followed by another email asking the same question should not receive the same credit as a complete, accurate response.

Include the cases the system hands back. A pilot that measures only the easy successes can look remarkably efficient while moving the difficult work onto someone else’s shift.

What does research tell us about AI productivity?

There is evidence of meaningful improvement, with important limits.

The published study Generative AI at Work examined 5,172 customer-support agents and found a 15% average increase in issues resolved per hour with AI assistance. Effects varied substantially across workers. The authors also observed small quality declines among the most skilled agents. This was a business-software company’s support operation, not a hotel trial. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025.

The practical lesson is to examine the actual work and the people doing it. A result from customer support is a reason to test a related hospitality application, not a percentage to paste into next year’s hotel budget.

Compare new and experienced employees. Separate routine questions from exceptions. Check whether the tool helps the night shift as much as the day shift. An average can conceal where training works, where the workflow is poorly designed and where human judgment remains essential.

How do you separate saved time from saved money?

Start with the arithmetic, then ask what happened to the hours.

Here is a fictional planning example, not a client result:

A reservations team handles 1,000 eligible inquiries a month. The baseline averages six minutes of staff work per inquiry. With AI assistance, the measured average is four minutes, including review, corrections and follow-up work.

The difference is 2,000 minutes, or about 33.3 staff hours a month.

At an illustrative fully loaded labor rate of $30 an hour, that represents about $1,000 of capacity value. If ongoing software, administration and support cost $600 a month, the apparent capacity-value surplus is $400 before setup costs.

Now the question that matters: did the hotel actually reduce an expense?

If payroll and overtime stayed the same, there is no demonstrated $1,000 cash saving. The team has time available for other work. That can be valuable, particularly if calls were going unanswered or service was rushed. Describe the benefit accurately and measure where the capacity goes.

If the hotel instead verifies $300 less overtime, report $300 of cash savings. Against $600 in ongoing monthly costs, that leaves a $300 monthly cash shortfall before setup costs and any other verified financial benefits. If the released capacity helps generate additional profitable bookings, estimate that contribution separately using a defensible comparison. Do not count the same hour as both eliminated labor cost and time spent producing more revenue.

Which costs belong in an AI business case?

The subscription is one line.

Add implementation, integration, data preparation, training, paid staff time, supervision, usage charges, maintenance and the work of keeping property information current. Include correction and recovery costs where they can be measured. Establish an exit plan for failed integrations or an underperforming vendor.

Use the same period on both sides of the calculation:

ROI (%) = [(verified financial benefits minus total AI costs) divided by total AI costs] × 100%.

State how one-time costs are treated. A first-year calculation should include the setup investment attributable to that year. A separate ongoing calculation can help explain future operating economics, but should not erase the initial spending.

Keep capacity benefits visible beside the financial calculation. Forcing every operational improvement into an invented cash number makes the case less credible.

For revenue applications, use incremental contribution after relevant costs rather than gross booking value. Consider cancellations, discounts, commissions, payment costs and fulfillment. A fuller hotel can still produce a disappointing return if acquiring and serving the extra business consumes the benefit.

How do you know AI caused the improvement?

Start measurement before deployment whenever possible. Keep definitions and data collection consistent.

Where appropriate, compare similar eligible work with and without the tool during the same period. Random assignment can strengthen the comparison when feasible and safe. A staged rollout can also provide useful evidence, although differences between teams, properties and periods need attention.

Account for changing inquiry volume, staffing, experience, occupancy, rates and demand. Avoid routing every easy case to the AI group and then declaring that the tool made the work easier.

Measure for long enough to include normal variation and a reasonable learning period. Set the decision date according to the volume and risk of the workflow. Thirty calendar days with twelve eligible cases may provide less evidence than one busy week elsewhere.

If the available comparison cannot support a causal claim, say so. An observed improvement can justify further testing without being presented as proven ROI.

When should a hotel scale, change or stop a pilot?

Agree on the decision rules before enthusiasm takes over.

Scale when the workflow shows repeatable benefit, meets quality requirements and has an owner able to maintain it.

Change when the opportunity is plausible but errors, adoption barriers or integration gaps explain weak results. Change one important element at a time where practical, then retest.

Stop when the workflow remains uneconomic, creates unacceptable risk or moves more work downstream than it removes.

Set clear boundaries for sensitive and consequential situations. An attractive average handling time should never excuse misleading accessibility information, unauthorized commitments or unsafe advice. Staff need a clear escalation route and the ability to intervene.

Stopping an unsuccessful pilot can be evidence of good management. Continuing because the launch presentation looked impressive is a more expensive habit.

What belongs in the next ownership report?

For each pilot, bring five things: the business problem, the baseline, the observed change, the full cost and the next decision.

Add the limits of the evidence. Show where recovered time went. Explain what guests and employees experienced, including the problems.

The invoice will still be easy to find. The result should be, too.

Common questions

Does higher AI usage prove ROI?

No. Usage establishes adoption. ROI requires financial benefits and costs. Usage is helpful for diagnosing why a tool is or is not affecting the workflow.

Can saved staff time count as a benefit?

Yes, as recovered capacity. It becomes a cash saving only when an expense is actually reduced or avoided. If the time improves service or supports additional business, measure that outcome separately.

What is a good ROI target for hotel AI?

There is no universal target. Set a hurdle that reflects the hotel’s alternatives, total costs, implementation risk and evidence quality. Establish minimum service and accuracy requirements alongside any financial target.
