AI Literacy for Librarians: Why I Pressed Pause in 2026

Michael J. Goldrich · January 9, 2026

Choosing to Be the Change as We Navigate This Technological Shift Together

AI literacy for librarians became my focus for the first week of 2026. Not because a client requested it. Not because it advanced a revenue target. Because I made a deliberate choice to press pause on corporate consulting and give back to the community we all share.

I spent the holidays reflecting on what kind of year I wanted 2026 to be. The easy path would have been diving straight into enterprise AI implementations, helping corporations beat their quarterly targets using the latest technology. That work pays well. It produces measurable outcomes. It fills a calendar quickly.

But something kept nagging at me. A question I could not shake: what kind of citizen do I want to be?

We are all in this together. Every technological shift in history has created winners and losers, and AI threatens to accelerate that division faster than anything we have seen before. Some people have access to AI literacy training through their employers. Others have the time and resources to teach themselves. But millions of people in our communities have neither. They hear about AI on the news, from grandchildren, from anxious coworkers. They sense that something important is happening. And they have no idea how to navigate it.

I decided to be the change I want to see happen. I designed a three-session, six-hour training program and delivered it to librarians at a large public library system during the first week of January. No corporate sponsor. No enterprise contract. A conscious decision to share what I know with the people best positioned to strengthen our community from within.

The Organizational Imperative: Why Libraries Must Lead

My primary work involves helping corporations drive revenue, optimize their AI implementations, and ensure their businesses get discovered by chatbots and AI search engines. Those engagements involve executive stakeholders, substantial budgets, and clear ROI metrics. This library training offered none of those things.

I chose it anyway. Because being a good member of my community matters more than another line item on a consulting invoice.

Libraries occupy a unique position in the AI ecosystem. They serve as the last non-commercial space where citizens can access information without algorithmic manipulation or profit motives. Research from the American Library Association indicates that 77% of Americans visited a public library in the past year, making these institutions one of the few remaining universal touchpoints in our fragmented society. When more than 700 million people use ChatGPT weekly (according to OpenAI’s December 2025 statistics), many of those users are library patrons seeking guidance they cannot find elsewhere.

The organizational mandate for AI literacy for librarians extends beyond patron service. Libraries face an existential question: will they become irrelevant artifacts of a pre-AI era, or will they evolve into essential navigators of information trustworthiness? The answer depends entirely on whether library staff can transform from gatekeepers of physical collections into guides for digital discernment.

Consider the competitive landscape libraries face. Google provides instant answers. ChatGPT generates seemingly authoritative responses. YouTube tutorials offer visual instruction. Against these always-available, algorithmically-optimized alternatives, libraries must offer something different: human judgment, contextual wisdom, and the ability to teach patrons how to evaluate information rather than simply consume it.

This is not a defensive strategy. This is libraries positioning themselves as the essential bridge between AI capability and human critical thinking.

The Departmental Reality: Building a Train-the-Trainer Framework

The training program I designed followed a deliberate progression across three two-hour sessions. Each session built on the previous one, moving staff from understanding to application to leadership.

Session One: Understanding and Experiencing AI

The first session established foundational comprehension. But I did not begin with definitions or technical explanations. I started with a pair activity: turn to the person next to you and share one thing you have heard about AI. The responses revealed everything about the challenge ahead.

“My nephew uses it for homework and I worry he is cheating.”

“I heard it makes things up.”

“Someone told me it will replace my job.”

Fear dominated the conversation. Skepticism ran deep. Several participants had never touched ChatGPT or any generative AI tool. These were information professionals with decades of experience, and they approached AI like it was radioactive.

This skepticism, I would later realize, made them the perfect students.

The session introduced three patron-ready analogies for explaining AI:

The Confident Stranger: Imagine asking a well-dressed stranger at a party a question. They answer immediately, confidently, and thoroughly. But you have no idea if they actually know what they are talking about. They might be an expert. They might be completely making things up. AI operates the same way.

The Sophisticated Parrot: A parrot can repeat phrases with perfect pronunciation without understanding their meaning. AI does something similar at a vastly more complex scale. It predicts what words should come next based on patterns in its training data, without comprehending the concepts those words represent.

Autocomplete on Steroids: Everyone has used autocomplete on their phone. AI extends that principle to paragraphs, essays, images, and code. It predicts what should follow based on what came before, but at a scale and sophistication that makes the outputs appear intelligent.

The hands-on component, which I called the “AI Petting Zoo,” gave each participant direct experience with ChatGPT and Google Gemini. Guided challenges demonstrated both capabilities and limitations. One exercise asked participants to request information about a fictional book. AI confidently invented plot summaries, publication dates, and even fake reviews. The hallucination became visceral proof that AI output requires verification.

I introduced the REAL Test as a framework for evaluating any AI output:

R – Relevance: Does this actually answer my question or help with my task?

E – Evidence: What sources or reasoning support this output?

A – Accuracy: Can I verify the facts, dates, names, and claims?

L – Limitations: What might the AI have gotten wrong or missed?

Session Two: Applying AI in Library Work

The second session shifted from understanding to practice. Participants used AI to complete actual library tasks: drafting event descriptions, creating marketing copy, summarizing program data, and supporting grant applications.

Advanced prompting techniques transformed generic AI outputs into useful professional content:

Role-based prompting: Instructing AI to adopt a specific perspective (“You are a children’s librarian explaining a summer reading program to parents of reluctant readers”).

Structured output: Specifying exact formats and components (“Provide the response as a bulleted list with three main points and one action item for each”).

Iterative refinement: Treating the first AI output as a draft rather than a final product, then requesting specific improvements.

Chain-of-thought: Asking AI to work through problems step by step, which often produces more accurate and useful responses.

Constraint setting: Establishing clear boundaries (“Do not exceed 150 words. Use language appropriate for a sixth-grade reading level. Avoid library jargon”).

The critical shift in this session involved teaching participants to design their own patron lessons. Each participant created a ten-minute micro-lesson following a consistent structure:

  • Minutes 0-2: Hook (why should patrons care about this?)
  • Minutes 2-5: Core concept (what is the one thing they need to understand?)
  • Minutes 5-8: Hands-on practice (let them try it themselves)
  • Minutes 8-10: Takeaway (what can they do tomorrow?)

These micro-lessons became the delivery mechanism for AI literacy for librarians to reach their communities. A librarian teaching seniors about avoiding AI-generated scam emails. A children’s specialist helping parents guide homework help without enabling plagiarism. A reference desk professional showing job seekers how to use AI for resume feedback while maintaining authenticity.

Session Three: Leading and Sustaining AI Literacy

The final session addressed the harder challenge: sustainability. Training programs that end with a certificate and a handshake rarely produce lasting change. This session built frameworks for ongoing learning and community impact.

Deepfake detection received significant attention. I demonstrated increasingly sophisticated examples of AI-generated images, videos, and audio, then taught verification techniques that patrons could apply immediately. The exercise of identifying which presidential speech was real and which was AI-generated produced visible reactions. Participants who had dismissed deepfakes as science fiction recognized the immediate threat to information integrity.

The session established what I called “sandbox rules” for safe AI experimentation within library systems. These guidelines addressed questions that library leadership rarely considers:

  • When is it appropriate for staff to use AI in their work?
  • What patron data can never be entered into AI systems?
  • How should AI-assisted outputs be disclosed to patrons and stakeholders?
  • What happens when AI produces inappropriate or biased content during a patron interaction?

The final component involved drafting a conference presentation proposal. Each participant outlined how they would share their AI literacy journey at a state or regional library conference. This exercise served multiple purposes: it reinforced learning through teaching, it created accountability for implementation, and it positioned the library system as a regional leader in AI education.

The Employee Evolution: Librarians as Community Lighthouses

I conduct pre- and post-surveys for all training programs. The results from this library engagement exceeded anything I have seen in corporate environments.

Every single participant reported increased confidence in using AI tools. That outcome was expected.

What surprised me was the transformation in attitude. Participants who began the program convinced that AI would eliminate their jobs ended the program viewing AI as a tool that amplified their essential value. Librarians are trained in information evaluation, source verification, and critical thinking. These skills do not compete with AI. They complement it perfectly.

The skepticism that initially seemed like an obstacle became the program’s greatest asset. These participants questioned everything. They demanded evidence. They refused to accept AI outputs without verification. In other words, they modeled exactly the behavior they would teach their patrons.

One participant described the shift this way: “I came in thinking AI was going to make libraries obsolete. I leave understanding that AI makes librarians more necessary than ever. Someone has to teach people that a confident answer is not the same as a correct answer.”

This evolution matters for all of us, not only librarians. The upcoming generation faces a genuine risk of losing critical information literacy skills. When AI provides instant answers, the motivation to understand sourcing, citations, and verification diminishes. Children who grow up asking ChatGPT instead of learning how to research may never develop the skills to question what they read.

Librarians and teachers represent the lighthouses guiding our communities through this fog. They occupy unique positions to intervene before information passivity becomes permanent. Preschool storytime can include lessons about asking questions. Homework help can emphasize verification rather than answers. Job search assistance can distinguish between AI suggestions and human judgment.

For many in our communities, the future feels impossibly dark. Technology moves too fast. The changes feel too vast. The learning curve seems too steep. Librarians can be the light that shows another path forward. But only if someone helps them see their own potential first.

This is why AI literacy for librarians extends far beyond professional development. This is about preserving foundational skills for democratic citizenship. And it reminds me why pressing pause on corporate work to invest in community education represents exactly the kind of choice I want to make in 2026 and beyond.

The Community Impact: We Are All in This Together

Pressing pause on corporate work to serve my community produced something I did not anticipate: clarity about what matters.

My consulting practice focuses on revenue generation, market visibility, and enterprise AI implementation. Those engagements produce measurable business outcomes. This library training produced something different: connection. Belonging. The feeling that comes from being a citizen who contributes rather than a consultant who extracts.

Participants arrived on Day One looking grim. They represented a profession that has endured decades of existential threats. Budget cuts. Digital transformation. Declining circulation. The rise of AI felt like another nail in a coffin that had been under construction for years.

They left Day Three smiling. Not because I told them everything would be fine. Not because I minimized the challenges ahead. But because they discovered their profession had never been more relevant. In a world drowning in AI-generated content of uncertain accuracy, the ability to evaluate, verify, and teach information discernment represents a service no algorithm can provide.

Here is what I realized during those six hours of training: we need each other. The librarians need people who understand AI to share knowledge with them. The community needs librarians to translate that knowledge into accessible guidance. I need the reminder that professional success means nothing without community contribution.

I designed an extensive workbook for participants to reference after the training concluded. The workbook included the REAL Test framework, prompting templates, patron lesson structures, and resource guides for continued learning. It represented knowledge transfer rather than knowledge dependency. The goal was never to make the library system reliant on external consultants. The goal was to build internal capacity that would compound over time.

The three frameworks I introduced for critical evaluation of AI output served purposes beyond immediate application:

The REAL Test (described above) provides a systematic approach to any AI output.

The Billboard Test addresses privacy: would you be comfortable if this information appeared on a billboard with your name attached? If not, do not enter it into an AI system.

The Hallucination Check establishes verification as a non-negotiable habit: every factual claim from AI must be confirmed through an independent source before being shared or used.

These frameworks do not require technical expertise. They do not assume prior AI experience. They work for seniors worried about scam emails and for children wondering if they can trust their homework helper.

This is what being a good citizen looks like in 2026. Not waiting for someone else to solve the AI literacy gap. Not assuming corporations or governments will figure it out. Stepping forward with whatever expertise we have and sharing it freely with the people who need it most.

I am choosing to make a difference. I am choosing to be part of the change that needs to happen. And I am hopeful, genuinely hopeful, that if enough of us make similar choices, our communities will emerge from this technological transition stronger and more resilient than before.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is AI literacy for librarians and why does it matter in 2026?

The Answer: AI literacy for librarians equips library professionals with the knowledge and skills to understand, use, and teach AI tools while maintaining critical evaluation standards.

The Elaboration: Libraries serve as one of the last non-commercial information spaces in society, making librarian expertise essential for guiding patrons through AI-generated content. With over 700 million weekly ChatGPT users seeking guidance, libraries must evolve from physical collection gatekeepers to digital discernment navigators. AI literacy enables librarians to teach verification skills, identify misinformation, and help patrons distinguish between confident AI outputs and accurate information. This transformation positions libraries as essential community resources rather than obsolete institutions threatened by technology.

2. How can librarians explain AI to patrons who have never used it?

The Answer: Three patron-ready analogies make AI comprehensible: the Confident Stranger, the Sophisticated Parrot, and Autocomplete on Steroids.

The Elaboration: The Confident Stranger analogy describes asking a well-dressed stranger at a party a question, receiving an immediate confident answer, but having no way to know if they actually have expertise. The Sophisticated Parrot explains that AI repeats patterns from training data without understanding meaning, similar to a parrot pronouncing words perfectly. Autocomplete on Steroids extends the familiar phone autocomplete function to paragraphs and essays, making the technology feel less alien. These analogies work across age groups and technical comfort levels, providing librarians with flexible explanations for diverse patron populations.

3. What is the REAL Test for evaluating AI output?

The Answer: The REAL Test provides a four-part framework for evaluating any AI output: Relevance, Evidence, Accuracy, and Limitations.

The Elaboration: Relevance asks whether the output actually answers the question or helps with the task at hand. Evidence examines what sources or reasoning support the output. Accuracy requires verification of facts, dates, names, and specific claims through independent sources. Limitations considers what the AI might have gotten wrong or missed entirely. This framework scales verification effort to stakes: light checks for brainstorming, full verification for facts that will be shared publicly. Librarians trained in the REAL Test can apply it instantly and teach it to patrons of all ages.

4. How do AI hallucinations affect library patron services?

The Answer: AI hallucinations produce confident-sounding but fabricated information, including fake citations, invented statistics, and non-existent books that patrons may accept as accurate.

The Elaboration: Generative AI systems lack internal mechanisms for distinguishing between accurate and fabricated content, meaning they present invented information with the same confidence as verified facts. Librarians encounter patrons who have received AI-generated book recommendations for titles that do not exist or research citations that lead nowhere. Teaching patrons to expect and detect hallucinations represents a core AI literacy competency. The Hallucination Check framework establishes verification as a non-negotiable habit: every factual claim from AI must be confirmed through an independent source before being shared or used.

5. Why should libraries invest in AI training when budgets are limited?

The Answer: AI literacy training positions libraries as essential community resources rather than obsolete institutions, directly addressing the existential question of library relevance.

The Elaboration: Libraries compete against Google, ChatGPT, and YouTube for information-seeking patrons who have unlimited free alternatives. AI training enables libraries to offer something these platforms cannot: human judgment, contextual wisdom, and the ability to teach evaluation rather than answer delivery. Staff who understand AI can integrate it into existing services without additional technology budgets. Trained librarians become force multipliers, teaching patrons skills that reduce misinformation spread and improve community information resilience. The investment produces returns through increased patron engagement and strengthened institutional positioning.

6. How can librarians teach AI safety to senior patrons concerned about scams?

The Answer: The Billboard Test framework and practical demonstrations of AI-generated scam content help seniors understand privacy boundaries and recognize manipulation.

The Elaboration: The Billboard Test asks a simple question: would you be comfortable if this information appeared on a billboard with your name attached? If not, that information should never be entered into an AI system. Seniors benefit from seeing actual examples of AI-generated scam emails, deepfake videos, and voice cloning demonstrations. Practical verification exercises help seniors develop habits for checking unexpected requests, even when they appear to come from family members or trusted institutions. Librarians can design ten-minute micro-lessons specifically addressing the AI-powered threats seniors encounter most frequently.

7. What role do librarians play in preventing youth from losing critical thinking skills?

The Answer: Librarians serve as essential educators who can intervene before information passivity becomes permanent, teaching children to question rather than accept AI outputs.

The Elaboration: Children growing up with instant AI answers may never develop foundational research skills including sourcing, citation evaluation, and verification habits. Preschool storytimes can incorporate early lessons about asking questions. Homework help sessions can emphasize the difference between using AI as a tool and depending on it as an oracle. Summer reading programs can include AI literacy components that make verification feel engaging rather than burdensome. Librarians and teachers represent the primary bulwark against a generation that learns to consume answers without questioning them.

8. How can libraries measure the success of AI literacy programs?

The Answer: Pre- and post-surveys measuring confidence levels, attitude shifts, and practical skill application provide quantifiable evidence of program effectiveness.

The Elaboration: Effective measurement begins before training through baseline surveys assessing participant comfort with AI tools, concerns about job displacement, and current usage patterns. Post-program surveys capture confidence increases, attitude transformations, and specific skill acquisitions. Follow-up assessments at 30 and 90 days measure whether participants actually implemented what they learned and taught patrons. Qualitative measures including patron feedback, program attendance, and staff-initiated AI integration projects demonstrate sustained impact beyond initial training sessions.

9. What resources do librarians need to continue AI learning after formal training ends?

The Answer: LinkedIn Learning AI courses, NotebookLM for research assistance, and curated online resources provide ongoing skill development without requiring additional budget.

The Elaboration: LinkedIn Learning offers structured AI courses that many library systems can access through existing institutional subscriptions. Google’s NotebookLM helps librarians process research materials and prepare patron programming. Community resources including AI Pedagogy repositories provide lesson templates and activity ideas. The train-the-trainer model creates internal AI Champions who facilitate peer learning and ongoing skill development. Workbooks from initial training sessions serve as ongoing references for frameworks and prompting techniques that librarians can apply to new situations.

10. How does AI literacy for librarians connect to broader community resilience?

The Answer: Libraries trained in AI literacy become community anchors for information trustworthiness, strengthening democratic participation and reducing misinformation vulnerability.

The Elaboration: Communities where citizens cannot evaluate information accuracy become vulnerable to manipulation, polarization, and decision-making based on fabricated content. Libraries represent one of the few institutions trusted across political and demographic divides to provide unbiased information guidance. AI-literate librarians extend that trust into the new information landscape, teaching verification habits that protect individuals and strengthen collective resilience. This community impact extends beyond library walls as patrons share what they learn with family members, coworkers, and social networks. The investment in librarian AI literacy produces exponential returns through community education networks.
