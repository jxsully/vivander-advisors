Why your AI visibility score jumped the week ChatGPT changed its model

A model change is an instrument change. How Vivander records the break, refuses a delta it cannot defend, runs a control and a canary, and what to ask a vendor.

By Michael Goldrich, Vivander Advisors. Published September 27, 2026.

Your dashboard showed 44 last Monday. This Monday it shows 55, and nobody on your team changed a page, answered a review or bought a listing. The same week, ChatGPT moved its default model. Before anyone forwards the screenshot to the owner as good news, ask what was measured. An 11-point move with no work behind it is more likely a change in the ruler than a change in the hotel, and a hotel that celebrates the ruler will later apologize for it.

This piece explains comparability in plain language: what a model change does to a measurement, what a cohort change does, and what a monitoring platform has to record so the number on the page means the same thing it meant last month. Every Vivander fact below comes from the platform’s own methodology (Observed: framework v3.53 to v3.65).

A model change is an instrument change

A thermometer that reads 68 in the lobby is telling you two things: the temperature of the lobby and the calibration of the thermometer. Swap the thermometer and the reading can move with the lobby untouched. An AI platform is the thermometer here. When ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Claude or Google AI Overviews changes the model behind its consumer default, every answer it produces from that day is drawn from a different instrument.

The effect is not small, and it is not confined to model launches. Daily source overlap for the same prompt on the same platform sits at 0.34 to 0.42, and 9 to 28 percent of decisions change even at temperature zero (Schulte et al., 2026, and Kirsten et al., both cited in the Master Framework). Profound‘s own research reports that 40 to 60 percent of cited domains change monthly across answer engines (tryprofound.com, checked 2026-09-25). A model change adds a step on top of that background movement.

Vivander records the model version on every run. That single field is what makes the rest of this article possible, because a break you did not record is a break you cannot show.

A cohort change is a change of ruler

If the model is the thermometer, the prompt set is the ruler. A visibility score is a rate over a fixed set of questions, and the moment the questions change, the rate is measured against a different length.

Consider a property that scored 44 on 24 questions in August. In September the marketing lead adds six questions about weddings, because the new sales director cares about weddings, and removes four about business travel. The September score is 38. Nothing about the hotel’s visibility to a business traveler fell. The ruler changed, and the two numbers describe two different tests.

Vivander handles this with a locked core cohort of 24 unbranded buyer prompts, derived from the client’s own ICP vocabulary at the diagnostic, and the cohort is versioned. Every change records who made it, when, and why. New questions go into a watch rotation, where every additional prompt in the library is measured once every four cycles, one run per cell, so a new question is seen without touching the score. When the client and Vivander agree the cohort should change, it becomes version 2, and the series is cut at that point on purpose.

The recorded break

A model change inside a scoring window is flagged inside that window. Readers see the line, the label, and which runs landed on which side of it. The score is still computed, because the four-cycle window is the unit of measurement, and the reader is told which part of the window was measured on the old instrument and which on the new.

What the platform does not do is smooth the series. A smoothed line looks calmer and reads as a trend. The break is the truth, and the truth is that half the window came from one thermometer and half from another.

The refused delta

Across a cohort change with no computed bridge, the platform refuses to print a difference: the cell reads “not comparable: cohort v1 to v2” and carries no number. The reason is simple. A number on a page gets quoted, and a quoted number gets forwarded, and a forwarded number ends up in a board deck with the property’s name on it.

This rule was written from a real incident. A hospitality client’s prompt cohort was revised between two reports, and the difference between the two scores was reported to the client as a 19-point decline. The property had not declined 19 points; the test had changed. Nobody had lied, and the client still spent a week explaining a fall that never happened. The rule that came out of that week is the one above: no bridge, no delta, and a labeled cell where the number would have been.

An analyst who wants to compare across versions can compute a bridge on the overlapping prompts and label it as such. The platform will not do it silently.

The quarterly control run and what “instrument drift” means

Recording the break tells you when the instrument changed. It does not tell you how much of your movement since then is the instrument. For that, you need a control.

Once a quarter, the platform re-measures every client’s cohort against a pinned reference model, the same one each quarter, and publishes the result beside the live score. The difference between the pinned run and the live run is instrument drift: the movement a property would show if it had done nothing at all and only the platforms had changed. It is published beside the score, never inside it, so the live number stays a reading of the consumer default and the drift stays a reading of the instrument.

In practice, a general manager reads two lines. The live score says what a guest got this month on the settings a guest uses, and the drift line says how much of the month’s movement the instrument would have produced anyway. Movement above the drift is yours.

The daily fleet canary

There is a third way a number can move without the hotel moving: the capture can fail. A platform changes its page layout, a session times out, retrieval returns nothing, and a dashboard that does not know the difference records the empty answer as an absence. The score falls, and the hotel did nothing wrong.

Every day, the platform asks one control question on every platform, across the whole fleet of clients. If the canary comes back empty on Perplexity on a Tuesday, the platform knows Tuesday’s Perplexity captures are suspect before any client’s score is computed. A capture failure is never mistaken for a client’s visibility falling. The rule is cheap to run and it removes an entire class of false alarms.

Why most dashboards smooth this over

Tools built for enterprise marketing teams are good products for that reader. Profound is a well-funded platform with a real data asset: 1.9 billion or more licensed real-user prompts, ten engines in its agency guide, and a Series C of $96 million at a $1 billion valuation in February 2026 (tryprofound.com and checkthat.ai, checked 2026-09-25). Peec AI is a clean, well-liked monitor with 3,000 or more customers and screenshot audit trails on its daily captures (peec.ai and industry-lens.com, checked 2026-09-25).

Their reporting model is daily runs averaged into a trend. Profound averages daily runs and publishes no interval; Peec AI reports Visibility, Position and Sentiment with no published interval; AthenaHQ, Scrunch AI, Otterly.AI, Semrush and Ahrefs publish none (vendor pages, checked 2026-09-25). None of the vendor pages we reviewed describes a recorded model break, a refused delta across a prompt-set change, a quarterly control against a pinned model, or a fleet canary. That is not an accusation. A daily average on a large prompt set is a reasonable design for a team with an analyst who understands its noise. For a hotel with one marketing manager, the same design turns a model change into a story about the hotel.

A worked example (illustrative)

Every number here is invented for the walkthrough. A Manhattan independent hotel has a Visibility Rate of 44 percent over four cycles, plus or minus 2.5 points, band Structured. In week three of the next window, ChatGPT changes its default model. The platform records the version on every run from that day and flags the break inside the window.

Its next full-window score is 51. When the general manager opens the window, she sees two labeled halves. The first six runs per cell, on the old model, average 45, and the last six, on the new model, average 57. ChatGPT alone moved from 40 to 64; the other four platforms moved within their intervals. She clicks into the ChatGPT answers and reads that the new model is citing a city dining guide her restaurant has been listed in since March. The old model rarely retrieved it.

Two weeks later, the quarterly control run lands. On the pinned reference model, the same cohort reads 46. Instrument drift is published as plus 5 beside the live score of 51. Her reading to ownership: the hotel is up about 2 points on the reference instrument, which is inside the interval, and the new ChatGPT model retrieves a listing the hotel earned in March. The 7-point live gain is real for a guest using ChatGPT today. It is not evidence that the hotel improved 7 points. Both statements are true, and the platform let her make both.

In the same quarter, the sales director asks to swap four prompts. The cohort becomes version 2, and the comparison cell between the last v1 window and the first v2 window reads “not comparable: cohort v1 to v2.” She does not have to explain a decline that never happened, because no number was printed for her to explain.

What to ask your vendor

  1. Do you record the model version on every run, and can I see it on the answer?
  2. When a platform changes its model, what appears on my chart?
  3. If my prompt set changes, will the dashboard print a difference across the change, and on what basis?
  4. Do you run a control against a pinned model, and where is the drift published?
  5. How do you tell a capture failure from a real absence, and how would I know it happened?
  6. What is the interval on the number I am looking at, and how many runs sit behind it?

A vendor with good answers to all six will show them on a live account within a minute. One with a chart that never breaks is showing you a chart, and the chart may be the calmest thing in the building.

What to do with this

Before the next ownership meeting, pull up whatever you use to track AI visibility and find the last date a platform changed its model. If nothing on the page marks that date, treat every movement across it as unexplained, and say so in the deck. Then ask the six questions above.
