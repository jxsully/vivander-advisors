Mentioned, cited, recommended: the three words that decide whether AI sends you a guest

Three words describe every AI answer about your hotel or restaurant. What each one means, how it is decided, and why the same score can need opposite fixes.

By Michael Goldrich, Vivander Advisors. Published September 27, 2026.

Most AI visibility tools report one thing: whether your organization’s name appeared. Some add a position. A few add a sentiment. The Vivander platform reports three words, and only three, because those three describe every state an answer can put a property in, and each state calls for a different fix (Observed: platform methodology, framework v3.53 to v3.65).

Here are the definitions, a worked example of one question on five platforms, and the two most common patterns Vivander observes when a hotel or restaurant reads its first results.

The three definitions

Mentioned. The answer says the property’s name. That is the whole test. A property listed in a table of eight options is mentioned. One named in a sentence that says “avoid it on weekends” is mentioned. Sentiment is recorded separately, so a mention is a mention.

Cited. The answer links the property’s own website. Position in the answer decides the tier, never a rater’s opinion (Observed: platform methodology). A rater reading an answer and deciding whether a link “counts” is a judgment call that drifts between raters and between weeks. Position in the answer is a fact about the answer. The platform stamps the basis of every cited verdict on the row so a client can see how it was decided.

Recommended. The property is put forward as a pick with a reason. “For a rooftop bar near the park, the Harbor House is the strongest fit; guests praise the terrace at sunset” is a recommendation. Every recommended result is also a mentioned one. Recommended is a subset of mentioned, never a separate count, so the two never add to more than 100 percent of runs.

One rule sits under all three. Branded questions are never scored. “Is the Harbor House good for a wedding?” will produce the Harbor House in the answer because the name is in the question. Those questions are recorded, and they are useful for a different purpose (the companion piece on brand recognition explains it). The visibility score is built on unbranded buyer questions only.

One question, five platforms (illustrative)

The question: “boutique hotel in the West Village with a garden courtyard for a wedding of 60.” Illustrative property: the Harbor House, an independent hotel with a courtyard that seats 60. Everything below is illustrative and constructed to show the shape of a reading.

PlatformResultWhat the answer did
ChatGPTRecommended, citedNamed three hotels with a sentence each. Put the Harbor House first, described the courtyard, linked the hotel’s own weddings page.
PerplexityRecommended, not citedNamed the Harbor House second with a reason drawn from a city wedding guide. Linked the guide. Did not link the hotel.
Google GeminiMentioned onlyListed six hotels in a table. The Harbor House appears as a row with an address. No reason given.
ClaudeAbsentNamed four hotels. The Harbor House was not among them. Two of the four have courtyards; two do not.
Google AI OverviewsMentioned, citedNamed the Harbor House in a list of five, with a link to its homepage. No reason given.

Five platforms, four different states, one hotel. A tool that reports “appeared on 4 of 5” has told the GM something true and nothing useful. The reading that matters is the pattern: recommended where the AI had a reason available, mentioned where it had a name and nothing else, absent where the sources it drew on did not include the hotel at all.

The sources column, which the platform records for every run, tells the rest. ChatGPT drew on the hotel’s own weddings page. Perplexity drew on a city wedding guide that lists the hotel. Gemini drew on a hotel directory that lists the hotel with an address and no amenities. Claude drew on two venue directories that do not list the hotel at all.

Two patterns, two different problems

Mentioned, never recommended

The AI knows the property exists. It has the name, an address, perhaps a category. What it does not have is a reason. In the answers Vivander reviews, a recommendation arrives with a sentence of justification, and the justification comes from somewhere: a stated fact on the property’s site, a line in a review, a description in a directory (Observed: Vivander audits, 2026).

A property that is mentioned in half its runs and recommended in none has a facts problem. Its website may describe “an unforgettable setting” where a rival’s states “courtyard seats 60, ceremony and reception, in-house catering.” The reviews may be strong on service and silent on the courtyard. Directory listings may be a name and a phone number.

The fix is publishing. State the facts, in plain text, on the page a crawler reaches. Fill the directory listings with the same facts. Where the reviews are silent on the thing you want to be recommended for, the diagnostic’s review benchmark shows what the rivals’ reviews say and where the gap is.

Recommended on Perplexity, absent on ChatGPT

The property has a reason. One platform found it. Another did not. In the worked example, Perplexity drew on a wedding guide that lists the Harbor House and Claude drew on two venue directories that do not. The property’s visibility on each platform is a function of which sources that platform leaned on for that kind of question, and whether the property stands on those sources.

The fix is a listing problem, and it is specific. For every prompt a rival won, the diagnostic’s influence ledger records which sources the AI leaned on and where the client stood on each (Observed: Vivander audit methodology, framework v3.61). In the wedding question’s row, the ledger names the two venue directories. The plan item is: get listed on those two, with the courtyard capacity stated. That is a different piece of work from “publish more content,” and it is a smaller one.

A GM reading the same score for both patterns would do the wrong work half the time. Three words instead of one is what prevents that.

Stability: the two-of-three reading

Every core prompt runs on every platform three times per weekly cycle, in three separate passes, so the three samples are not three readings of one cached state (Observed: platform methodology). Three runs produce a stability reading for each cell. A property that appears in two or three of the three runs reads as stable for that question on that platform. One that appears in one of three reads as intermittent.

Intermittent is its own finding. In our audits it tends to mean the property is at the edge of the shortlist: present when the AI draws on one set of sources, absent when it draws on another. The fix is corroboration. A fact stated on the site, confirmed on the Google Business Profile, repeated on the directory listings and echoed in reviews reads as established. One stated once reads as a claim, and a claim gets included intermittently.

Why the offered rate is over runs

The offered rate, the visibility component that anchors Layer 4 of the diagnostic score and the Visibility Rate in monitoring, is computed over runs rather than over prompts (Observed: platform methodology). A prompt where the property appeared once in three runs contributes one appearance in three, neither a win nor a loss. One where it appeared in all three contributes three in three.

The alternative, scoring each prompt as a yes or no, would treat the intermittent cell and the stable cell identically, and would report the property as more visible than a guest is likely to find it. Over a four-cycle window that is 12 runs per prompt-and-platform cell, and the rate carries a 95 percent Wilson interval of about plus or minus 2.5 points (Observed: Vivander monitoring review, 2026-09-05). What the guest is likely to see is what the number describes.

Why a recommendation outweighs a mention

On a search results page the traveler does the comparing. They open tabs, read reviews, check the map, and narrow eight options to one. An AI recommendation does that work for them. The model has already weighed location against price against review themes and handed back a conclusion. The traveler has no reason to redo the analysis. They accept the answer, because accepting the answer is why they asked.

Recommendations also compound. The traveler’s next prompt builds on the first: “What is the best room type there?” or “How far is it from the venue?” Once the model has chosen a property, the rest of the conversation is about that property. The competitors from the original list drop out of the exchange. A mention put you in the room. A recommendation closed the door behind you.

None of this shows up in Google Analytics. The exchange happens inside the AI interface. The traveler reads, compares and forms a preference before clicking anything. If they click at all, the session arrives tagged as direct, organic or referral, with no record that an AI answer shaped it.

What to do with this

Read your results in three columns, never one. For every unbranded question that matters to your organization, ask: mentioned where, cited where, recommended where, and absent where.

Where you are mentioned and never recommended, look at your own site first. Find the fact the AI would need in order to give a reason, and check whether the site states it in plain text.

Where you are recommended on one platform and absent on another, ask for the sources. Which directories and guides did the platform that found you draw on, and which did the platform that missed you draw on? The gap between those two lists is your listing work for the quarter.

And when the first result comes back as “mentioned on four of five,” do not stop there. Four mentions can be four different states.
