Why AI recommended your competitor instead of you: reading the influence ledger

For every question a rival won, the influence ledger shows the sources the AI leaned on, your state on each, and the reason given. How to read it and act on it.

By Michael Goldrich, Vivander Advisors. Published September 27, 2026.

A director of sales types the question a planner would type: “private dining room for 30 near the financial district with a set menu.” The answer puts forward three restaurants and hers is not one of them. At the top sits a room two blocks away that seats fewer and charges more. Only one thing from the report on her desk will satisfy her, and it is not a score. She wants the reason.

The influence ledger is the part of the Vivander Visibility Audit that answers her. For every prompt a rival won, it records which sources the AI leaned on, the client’s state on each of those sources, and the reason the rival was chosen. This piece explains what the ledger holds, the scoring behind it, and what a hospitality team does with a page of it. Every Vivander fact below comes from the platform’s own methodology (Observed: framework v3.53 to v3.65). The worked example is illustrative and labeled as such.

What the influence ledger records

Every scored run on the platform captures the raw answer, the tier (mentioned, cited or recommended), the sentiment, the citations, and the competitors the answer put forward. The ledger is built from those captures at the diagnostic, and it is organized by the prompts the client lost.

For each losing prompt, the ledger holds four columns.

  • Sources the answer leaned on: the pages the platform cited or drew from. A directory listing, a review site profile, a trade press feature, a city guide, or the rival’s own website stating a fact plainly.
  • The client’s state on each: listed or not listed. Reviewed or not reviewed. Featured or never covered. Stated on the client’s own site, or absent from it.
  • Position the rival took: first of three, second of five, the only pick.
  • Reason the answer gave: the sentence the AI used to justify the choice, quoted from the capture. “Private room for up to 40 with a set menu and AV” is a reason. “Highly rated” is a reason. The reason tells you which fact did the work.

Recorded, never scored, the ledger does not move the Live Visibility Score, and it is not a rating of the rival. Think of it as a map of where the answer came from, drawn so a marketing lead can see which square on the map is empty.

Off-site corroboration has three parts

The ledger reads more clearly once you know what the audit is measuring off the website. Layer 3 of the Visibility Stack Score is Off-Site Corroboration, worth 25 percent of the composite (Vivander benchmark: VSS layer weights). It has three parts, weighted 50, 30 and 20 (Vivander benchmark: VSS Layer 3 sub-weights).

  • Presence, 50: is the property on the surfaces that matter for its vertical? The audit runs a directory census by name, so a restaurant is checked against the named dining directories and reservation platforms for its market, and a hotel against the named travel and meetings directories. An absence becomes a row in the census.
  • Consistency, 30: do the surfaces agree with each other and with the property’s own site? Capacity, address, hours, price range, the name of the private room. In the audit, every absence claim is a regex over a capture, so “your Google Business Profile lists 25 seats and your site says 40” is backed by both captured pages.
  • Elaboration, 20: do the surfaces say anything beyond the listing? A review that describes the private room, a feature that names the chef, an owner reply that adds a fact. Presence is the bare listing; elaboration is the listing with a story.

The three parts explain a losing prompt. A rival wins the group-dining question because it is present on the directory the answer retrieved (presence), the capacity on that directory matches its site (consistency), and a trade feature described the room (elaboration). The client loses because one or more of those squares is empty.

Source Authority: earned media counts before AI cites it

One component of the Live Visibility Score covers this too. C6, Source Authority, is 10 percent of the live score (Vivander benchmark: LVS v2.0 component weights). It is scored from the off-site corpus, the set of third-party pages about the property collected in the audit, rather than from what the AI happened to cite in a given week.

The distinction matters for a hotel running a plan. If a regional trade title publishes a feature on your renovated ballroom in March, the AI platforms may not retrieve it until May, and one of them may never retrieve it. Scoring Source Authority from the corpus means the feature counts in March, when the work was done. A citation-only score would mean waiting for the platforms to notice, and punishing the property for the delay. The ledger and the component work together: the ledger shows which sources the answers leaned on last month, and the component shows whether the property’s own corpus is growing in the direction those sources point.

A worked example: the group-dining prompt (illustrative)

Every fact in this example is invented for the walkthrough. A Boston restaurant group owns a 120-seat restaurant with a private room that seats 30, a set menu at three price points, and a screen and microphone in the room. The room has hosted about 40 corporate dinners in the past year, and the rival two blocks away has a private room that seats 24.

Its core cohort includes the prompt “private dining room for 30 near the financial district with a set menu.” Over four cycles, across five platforms and twelve runs per cell, the rival is recommended in 43 of 60 runs and the client is mentioned in 4 and recommended in 0. The ledger for this prompt reads as follows.

Source the answer leaned onRival’s stateClient’s stateWhat the answer said
A city dining directory with a private-dining filterListed, capacity 24, set menu notedNot listed“Listed for private dining with a set menu”
An event-venue directory used by corporate plannersListed, room photos, AV notedNot listed“Offers AV in the private room”

The rival’s own websiteStates capacity, menu prices, AV, on one pageThe client’s site says “private dining available,” with no capacity, no prices, no AV“Seats up to 24 with a set menu from $95”

Read the reason column first. Three of the four reasons are facts the client’s room satisfies better than the rival’s. The client has more seats, the same menu structure and the same AV. None of those facts exists anywhere the answer looked. The client’s own site states one sentence. Neither directory has an entry. No trade title has been pitched.

The audit calls this row unsourced-but-true: a fact the client confirmed at intake that the website never states. It is the highest-yield finding in the What AI Knows About Your Business section because the fix is publishing, and publishing needs no third party.

What to do

The ledger converts to work in four moves, in this order, because each one is cheaper and faster than the next.

  • Publish the fact. Put the capacity, the price points, the AV and the room’s name on one page of your own site, in plain sentences, with a heading a planner would search for. In the example, the client’s private dining page goes from one sentence to a page that states 30 seats, three set menus with prices, a screen and a microphone, and a photo of the room set for dinner. That repairs the fourth row and gives every other source something to corroborate.
  • Earn the listing. Submit to the two directory types the answer leaned on: the city dining directory with the private-dining filter, and the event-venue directory the planners use. Use the same capacity, the same menu language and the same room name as the site, so the consistency part of Layer 3 scores clean. In the example, that repairs rows one and two.
  • Ask for the review. The next three corporate dinners each get a request for a review that mentions the private room by name. Elaboration comes from a guest describing the room, and the ledger shows that a described room is what the answer quoted.
  • Pitch the feature. Regional trade titles that profiled the rival in March cover private dining programs. The client has a story the rival does not: 40 corporate dinners in a year in a 30-seat room. A pitch with the number in the first line is the whole of the work. When the feature runs, Source Authority counts it from the corpus, before any platform cites it.

Each move maps to a finding code in the Implementation Plan, so the marketing lead, the web developer and the PR agency are working from the same line items. For a branded or managed property, each action also carries a Control tag saying whether the property or the brand owns that surface, so nobody spends a month on a page the brand controls.

How the weekly cycle shows whether it worked

The platform re-measures the locked core cohort every week: every core prompt, on every platform, three runs per cell in three separate passes. Because the group-dining prompt is one of the 24, it is measured 15 times a week and 60 times over a four-cycle window. The score moves when a cycle lands and never because a day passed.

In the example, the client publishes the page in week one and submits the two listings in week two. The directories publish the entries in week four. Over the four cycles that follow, the client’s tier on the prompt moves from mentioned in 4 of 60 to mentioned in 22 and recommended in 9. Since the interval at 12 runs per cell is about plus or minus 2.5 points on the Visibility Rate (Observed: monitoring review, 2026-09-05), the movement is outside it. The answers feed shows the two directory entries appearing in the citations from week five. No trade feature has run yet. When it does, the ledger for the next quarter will show a fourth source with the client’s state changed from never covered to featured.

If the number had not moved, the feed would show that too, and the ledger would show which source the answers were still leaning on. A plan that does not work is visible in the same place as a plan that does.

What to do with this

Take the last prompt you know a rival won. Write down, from memory, the three sources you think the answer used and where your property stands on each. Then check. The influence ledger replaces the memory with the capture. Vivander builds it at the diagnostic, and the platform shows whether the fixes worked, week by week.
