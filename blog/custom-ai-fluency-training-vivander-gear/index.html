Can Vivander customize AI fluency training around our team’s real work?

Michael J. Goldrich · September 11, 2026

Vivander Answers · 5 of 10

By Michael J. Goldrich, Founder and Chief Advisor, Vivander Advisors.

Yes. I build training around the roles, tasks and decisions your team needs to handle. Vivander’s GEAR methodology provides a foundation, while examples, practice and reinforcement should fit your organization. The goal is for employees to use AI with better judgment in real work, including checking and correcting outputs.

Why does a generic demonstration fall short?

A demonstration shows what a tool does. Employees still need to decide when to use it, what context to provide, how to evaluate the result and what information is appropriate to share. Those decisions vary by role and workflow.

A marketer drafting a brief, a manager reviewing research and an employee preparing a customer response have different quality requirements. Exercises should make those differences visible, rather than assuming everyone needs the same prompt or level of autonomy.

What should we define before training?

We identify the audience, approved tools, representative tasks, company constraints and behaviors you want to improve. Your team can provide examples with sensitive information removed or replaced appropriately. Materials and format are agreed in the training scope.

The learning objective should be observable. “Use AI confidently” is broad. “Prepare a draft from approved material, identify unsupported claims and correct it before sharing” gives participants something to practice and managers something to review.

How does GEAR fit the program?

GEAR is Vivander’s methodology for practical AI learning. I use it as a foundation for repeatable habits while tailoring the program to the team’s work. The training page describes current formats and the approach.

Customization should affect tasks, examples, discussion and follow-through, rather than only adding a company logo to slides. We can distinguish skills everyone needs from specialized exercises for particular teams.

What does a useful exercise look like?

Consider a fictional employee preparing an explanation from approved policy material. The exercise can require the employee to specify the audience, provide context, inspect the draft, identify unsupported statements and revise the answer. The output and review process both matter.

A deliberately included plausible error in sample material can help participants practice noticing problems. The exercise should reward judgment as well as speed. Real deployment still follows your company’s tool and data policies.

What should managers reinforce?

Managers can ask employees to explain why they chose AI for a task, which sources they used and what they checked before sharing the result. They can review a small set of completed examples and discuss corrections without treating every mistake as a reason to stop learning. The review should fit the work and company policy. A clear standard for acceptable output gives employees something concrete to aim for after the workshop ends.

How do we measure progress after a workshop?

Use comparable tasks and an evaluation rubric, with a baseline where practical. We can review task selection, context, evaluation, responsible application and repeatability. The AI fluency measurement guide explains those dimensions.

Reinforcement can include follow-up practice, manager discussions and reviewed examples, depending on the engagement. Attendance alone does not show people can use AI well. The useful test is whether learning transfers to work the company needs done.

Related questions

Can you train departments differently?

Yes. The program can distinguish shared skills from role-specific tasks, with audiences, exercises and formats agreed in scope.

Do employees need programming skills?

The required background depends on the program. Practical business training can focus on task selection, context, evaluation and responsible use.

Is workshop attendance proof of fluency?

No. Attendance differs from demonstrated skill. Comparable work exercises and follow-up review provide better evidence of progress.
