A family is planning a week at the beach. One parent opens Google, types “best resorts for families” with the name of the beach town, and reads the AI summary at the top of the page. Six resorts are listed, each with a line about pools and kids’ programs. The summary closes by asking what kind of family vibe they want.The source audit examined a luxury resort with established third-party credentials. On the family-resort question described in the paper, Google AI Overviews omitted it on all three runs. Google AI Mode, ChatGPT and Gemini also omitted it. The hotel, its brand, location and identifying details are withheld.Nobody at the resort will ever see that answer. No inquiry arrived, so sales has nothing to log. Revenue sees no denial. Every report the resort receives will look the same as last month, because the conversation that removed it from consideration happened somewhere none of those tools look.Hotels have spent twenty years building instruments to measure the moment a guest reaches the website. Almost none of them measure what happened before.An AI visibility audit measures it. Ongoing monitoring keeps measuring it. What follows is the case for both, built on a real Vivander audit of a branded luxury beachfront resort collected in the summer of 2026. The resort, its brand, its location, and its competitors are not named, and review counts are rounded. Every other figure comes directly from the audit.

Why should a luxury retailer assess AI visibility?

The proposal is modest. Commission a baseline audit for one property, built around the guest segments that carry the most revenue. Test how six AI platforms describe and recommend the hotel across about a hundred realistic guest questions, three times each. Log every factual error. Compare the results with the competitive set. Turn the findings into a ranked list of corrections with owners, then re-test at thirty and ninety days to confirm what changed.The assessment provides information the hotel does not otherwise collect: whether the property appears for guest needs it serves, whether the facts are right, and which sources appear alongside competing recommendations.Every one of those questions has an answer today. The only open matter is whether anyone at the hotel has read it.

The Shortlist Now Forms Before the Visit

Guest behavior has moved past speculation, and the numbers are large enough to plan around.Pew Research Center surveyed 5,119 U.S. adults in February 2026 and found that 42 percent use AI chatbots to search for information, the most common reason people gave for using chatbots at all. Sixty percent said they read the AI summaries that now sit at the top of search results.[1] These are self-reported figures about any use, and they say nothing yet about hotel bookings in particular. They do establish that a conversational answer has become an ordinary place to start.Travel is where the commercial signal is clearest. Adobe’s August 2026 traffic report found that AI-referred visits to U.S. travel sites grew 119 percent year over year in July. Those visitors converted at a rate only 1 percent below visitors from all other sources, the narrowest gap Adobe has recorded since October 2024, and they stayed on site 67 percent longer.[2] Adobe’s figures are an industry benchmark across many travel businesses, so no single hotel should assume it will match them. What they retire is the assumption that AI traffic is idle browsing with no intent behind it.The deeper change is structural. When a guest types a keyword, the search engine returns ten options and the guest does the comparing. Describe a need instead, and the platform does much of the comparing on her behalf, then returns a short answer, often a shortlist of a handful of properties. A hotel can rank well for every term its agency tracks. That ranking guarantees nothing about the answer, because the answer is assembled from different material.

Why Current Reports Cannot See It

Web analytics, rank tracking, review monitoring and reputation dashboards each answer useful questions. They do not provide a complete record of what AI answers tell guests before they visit the website. The assessment examines those answers directly.Ahrefs tested that gap directly. Its data team ran 15,000 long-tail queries through Google and Bing, put the same questions to ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, and Perplexity, and checked whether the URLs the assistants cited appeared in the search results. Across ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot, only about 12 percent of cited URLs ranked in Google’s top ten for the same question, and 80 percent did not rank anywhere in Google for it.[3] The study dates from August 2025 and the platforms have changed since, so treat the exact figure as directional. Its underlying finding has held: the sources an AI answer draws on are largely different from the pages a rank report tracks.The audit observed four negative sources cited in AI answers outside Google’s top ten for the tested queries: three critical reviews on a major travel review site and a local review-platform listing. The most critical review appeared on at least two of three Google runs of complaint questions. Source titles and identifying details are withheld. Search rank alone did not capture these observations.A review of search rankings and a review of AI answers can therefore reveal different issues. Inspect the answer evidence alongside the existing reports.AI-influenced visits can arrive without a referrer and appear in the Direct channel. Attribution is incomplete, and this audit did not quantify the missing share. Read the answers directly and keep referral analytics as partial evidence.

What the Audit Found

Method matters as much as findings here, because a single screenshot of a single chatbot response proves very little. In September 2026, researchers from Maastricht University and two partner universities repeated 117 shopping questions three times across five AI interfaces. Repeat runs of the same question on ChatGPT shared only about a quarter of their cited domains, and ChatGPT and Gemini, asked the identical question, shared about 5 percent.[4] The study covered physical products in one country, which limits how far it transfers to hotels. Its lesson for any audit is direct. One answer is an anecdote. Patterns require repetition.The resort’s audit was built on that lesson. It asked 102 questions written in the voices of the resort’s actual buyers, none of them naming the property, plus 24 questions that did. Every question ran three times on each of six platforms: ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, Gemini, and Google AI Mode. The Branded Hotel’s Particular Problem

This resort operates under a global brand, and that changes who can fix what. Layers 1 and 2 of the audit, 55 percent of the composite score, were measured mostly on the brand’s website, which the property cannot edit.The brand pages put real friction in front of machines. Its most cited page refused automated fetches. Restaurant and bar pages served template text with no outlet name. The markup carried no rooms, rates, room count, or awards. On mobile, the booking path from an AI answer scored 30 out of 100, with the booking widget defaulting to tonight and one adult. And the brand’s robots file blocked crawlers it did not name from the booking entry, so an AI agent that obeys the file can read the property pages and cannot follow the path to a rate.The business case for a branded property rests on what comes next. Most of what decided a recommendation sat off the brand site, and the property controls it now.Exhibit 6

Why the Audit Needs Monitoring

An audit is a photograph. The answers it captures will change, sometimes because the hotel fixed something and sometimes because a platform changed how it retrieves sources. Monitoring turns the photograph into a record.The source audit proposed a targeted thirty-day re-test, a broader ninety-day comparison after the first corrections and brand requests, and ongoing checks of the agreed questions. Timing depends on the work, source updates and platform behavior. A fixed improvement date cannot be promised.Exhibit 8

Neither event announces itself, and both are visible only to someone who keeps asking.

What the Audit Will Not Do

Credibility depends on stating the limits as plainly as the findings, and this audit published its own.Analytics, Search Console, and the Google Business Profile manager were not part of the engagement, so nothing was estimated in their place.The results describe what the tested platforms said, on the tested dates, in response to the tested questions. They do not estimate how many real guests saw those answers, and they cannot be converted into lost revenue. Any provider offering that conversion is inventing an input.No one can guarantee a recommendation. Platforms change how they retrieve and rank sources without notice. What can be agreed in advance is the scope, the method, the quality of the evidence, and the process for acting on it.The audit does not replace search work either. Google’s own guidance, updated in July 2026, states that its generative AI features are rooted in its core ranking and quality systems, and that no special AI files or markup are required to appear in them.[5] Sound SEO remains the foundation. Google also began rolling out Search Console reporting on how pages appear in AI Overviews and AI Mode in June 2026 and extended it to all websites by the end of August.[6] Hotels should use it. That reporting covers Google’s surfaces only. The audit compares six selected AI experiences and reads the claims and cited sources in their answers. Use the platform reporting alongside that evidence.

Measuring Success

A credible program reports a small number of measures, each with its denominator: the named rate by platform and segment, recommendation events, and material factual errors found, corrected at source, and still present at each re-test. It also tracks the negative sources AI cites and the actions completed against the plan.Alongside those sit the business measures the hotel already follows. AI referral sessions in analytics, inquiries and RFPs by segment, and bookings where attribution allows all belong in the review. Treat them as partial evidence. A rise in wedding inquiries after the venue categories go live is encouraging. It is not proof, and the report should say so.One instrument is deliberately absent. Accuracy and reputation never fold into a single composite score, because a score of that kind rewards invisibility. A hotel that appears in no answers has no wrong facts and no negative mentions, and it would score perfectly. The audit delivers a work list instead, which is more useful than a number that improves when things get worse.

How should your organization start?

Choose one property and the two or three guest segments that carry the most revenue per booking. Agree on the competitive set, the platforms, and who at the hotel will own each category of fix before the first question runs. For a branded property, separate the fixes the hotel can make this week from the requests that go to the brand. Fix the facts that are already true first. Re-test at thirty and ninety days, and let the results decide how much monitoring is justified after that.Hospitality has always known that the most important conversations about a hotel happen when the hotel is not in the room. A guest recommends it to a colleague. Planners describe it to their bosses. For twenty years those conversations moved onto review sites, and the industry learned to read them.The newest version of that conversation takes place between a guest and a machine. It is also the first one a hotel can read in advance, by asking the same questions its guests ask.
