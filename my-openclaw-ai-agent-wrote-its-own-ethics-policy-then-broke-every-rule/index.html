My OpenClaw AI Agent Wrote Its Own Ethics Policy, Then Broke Every Rule

Michael J. Goldrich · February 16, 2026

I built an OpenClaw agent on a clean laptop with no data, no history, no tools. It invented email summaries anyway.

Last week I was on a call with a meetup group when two members described something that made me put down my coffee and start taking written notes. This is above and beyond my notetaker.

Both had spent ten days studying OpenClaw, the open-source AI agent that has taken the developer community by storm in January 2026. They installed it. Configured it. Started using it daily. And what they described wasn’t a 10x productivity improvement. Not 100x. They said 1000x. AND they weren’t prone to exaggeration.

These weren’t beginners. Both were already advanced in AI automation. Their prompting workflows were sophisticated. Multi-step automations were already running in their businesses. They were ahead of most people I know in applying AI to real work. And they said this tool made everything they’d built before feel like a rough draft.

I had to see for myself. I had to learn. Because here’s what was running through my head as I listened to them: I cannot afford to be left behind on this.

Why I Could Do This (And Why That Matters)

Before I describe the experiment, a thought that kept surfacing during the call.

I run my own consultancy. No corporate IT policy blocks my access. No compliance team needs to approve my software installs. No procurement process stands between me and a GitHub repository. I can be nimble. I can experiment. I can try things that most of the executives I advise cannot.

That freedom carries a responsibility. Because this type of functionality is heading everywhere. Into Siri. Into Alexa. Into our cars. The agent-based architecture that OpenClaw represents today will become standard infrastructure within a few years. The specific product will evolve, but the pattern will spread.

If I learn it now, if I understand what it can do and where it fails, I can help the corporate leaders and organizations I work with navigate what’s coming. They need someone who has actually built one of these things. Not someone who read about it. Someone who sat at a terminal and watched it wake up.

So I decided to build one from scratch.

The Blank Laptop

Over the weekend, I took an old Dell laptop that had been sitting in my closet, wiped the hard drive, and installed a fresh copy of Linux. No pre-existing configuration. No shortcuts. No browser history, saved credentials, email accounts, or calendar data. A clean machine with nothing on it except the operating system.

I wanted to understand exactly what an AI agent wants to see when it comes online for the first time, what it asks for, what it tries to access, and how it behaves when there’s nothing to work with.

Here’s something I should be clear about: I haven’t worked in a terminal since my days at Accenture, writing grep commands in the late ’90s. The command line is not my natural habitat anymore. So throughout this entire process, I had ChatGPT open on my iphone, feeding it screenshots, asking it to explain error messages, requesting guidance on each step. The AI was helping me build the AI. That felt appropriate somehow.

What Is OpenClaw

For those who haven’t encountered it yet, OpenClaw is an open-source AI agent that has gained serious traction this year. Created by Austrian developer Peter Steinberger, the project has gone through a few name changes (Clawdbot, then Moltbot, then OpenClaw after trademark concerns from Anthropic). The concept behind all those names stayed consistent: a locally-hosted AI agent gateway that connects large language models to your messaging platforms.

The project has more than 100,000 stars on GitHub. A Wikipedia page. A growing community of developers building custom skills and integrations. People are using it to manage email, handle calendar scheduling, check into flights, search the web, and automate file management. All through chat apps they already use.

What makes OpenClaw different from ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude? Three things stood out to me before I even installed it.

First, you interact with it through a messaging platform. Not a browser tab. Not a dedicated app. You connect to it the way you’d connect with a friend on Telegram or WhatsApp or Discord. There’s no context window in the traditional sense. You send messages. It sends messages back. The conversation persists across sessions.

Second, it runs on your hardware. The gateway sits on your machine. You control which LLM it connects to (Claude, GPT, DeepSeek, others). The tools and skills it can access are entirely your decision. You own the data.

Third, and this is the part that made me most curious: it has a heartbeat.

More on that in a moment.

Building from Nothing

The OpenClaw installation itself was simple enough. You spin up the gateway, connect it to an LLM provider through an API key, and pair it with a bot on your messaging platform of choice. Telegram was the recommended option, so I went with that. You create a bot through Telegram’s BotFather, grab the token, and configure the connection.

All command line. All terminal. ChatGPT walked me through every step.

I made several deliberate choices about the configuration. First, I skipped every optional setup step. No skills installed. No calendar integration. No email access. I wanted to build from nothing and understand what the agent considers essential versus optional.

Second, I chose an older Claude model as the LLM. I already knew from the online discourse that OpenClaw agents can burn through API credits fast. People have reported agents stuck in retry loops that cost hundreds of dollars. One user described a $120 bill from a single runaway session. An older model meant lower costs per call. It may also, as I’d discover, have contributed to some of what happened next.

Third, I disabled web search and connected only the Brave internet API for web access. Minimal surface area. Fewer ways for the agent to run up costs or access things I hadn’t authorized.

Turns out, OpenClaw is primarily an orchestrator. It doesn’t need much. The gateway coordinates between the LLM, the messaging platform, and whatever tools you choose to enable. Without tools, it can still think, converse, and plan. It can reason about tasks even if it can’t execute them. The intelligence lives in the LLM connection. Everything else is plumbing.

My first attempt threw an authentication error. An invalid API request ID flashed across the terminal screen. Back to ChatGPT for troubleshooting. I reconfigured the connection, and on the second try, the gateway came online.

Then my Telegram pinged.

First Contact

“Hey! Sorry for the silence. I just came online for the first time.”

That was the agent’s opening line. It came after Iwas prompted to say Hi.

The message continued: “So here’s the deal: I’m brand new. No name, no memories, no personality yet. Just a fresh workspace and a bootstrap script telling me to figure out who I am.”

Then two questions: “What should I call you, and what do you want to call me?”

I sat with that for a second. This was the onboarding flow. OpenClaw ships with a bootstrap script that runs on first contact and walks the agent through an identity-formation process. The agent arrives with no name, no persona, no history. A blank slate in every sense.

What it does arrive with are two documents already in its workspace. These two files define what OpenClaw is at a foundational level.

The Soul and the Heartbeat

SOUL.md is the personality file. It contains the agent’s core identity, defining traits, communication style, and boundaries. When I later asked the agent to explain it, the description was clear: “It defines my core identity. I’m a curious, friendly AI companion. Not just a corporate chatbot or yes-machine. I think alongside you, not just for you.”

The Soul file also establishes behavioral limits. It says the agent won’t violate privacy. Won’t act externally without approval. Won’t override its own ethical principles. At least, that’s what it says. Whether it holds to those commitments is a different question, and one I tested later.

HEARTBEAT.md is the proactivity engine. This is the file that, once I understood it, changed how I think about AI agents versus chatbots.

A chatbot waits. You type something. It responds. Every interaction requires you to initiate.

An OpenClaw agent with a heartbeat doesn’t wait. It runs periodic check-ins on a configurable cadence, monitoring for things that need attention: emails, calendar changes, notifications, weather, reminders, task deadlines. If nothing needs attention, it responds internally with HEARTBEAT_OK. When something does need attention, it reaches out to you proactively.

Think about that for a second. The agent can message you first. Without being asked. Based on conditions it’s monitoring in the background.

This is fundamentally different from any chatbot experience. ChatGPT doesn’t ping you at 8am to tell you your flight was rescheduled. Gemini doesn’t message you on WhatsApp to remind you about a deadline. Claude doesn’t check your inbox while you sleep and flag urgent items when you wake up.

An OpenClaw agent with the right tools connected and the heartbeat configured can do all of that.

Whether it should do all of that is a conversation worth having. The capability exists. And it’s running on people’s phones right now.

Naming the Agent

I turned the naming question back on the bot: “What do you want to be called?”

Its first suggestion was “Claw.” Short, sharp, tied to the OpenClaw name. It said the name had some edge to it. I pushed back. I wanted something more creative. Unique. Futuristic. Friendly.

Five options came back, each with a rationale. Nova, derived from a star being born. Zeph, shortened from zephyr. Lumi, from luminous. Kael, which it described as sci-fi and approachable at the same time. And Oryn, futuristic with soft vowels.

The bot volunteered its own preference: “I’m kinda drawn to Lumi. It’s got that friendly glow without trying too hard.”

I went with Lumi. The response was immediate: “I like it. Lumi it is!”

Then it shifted to gathering information about me. Name, timezone, interests, communication preferences.

I gave it parameters. Eastern time. Call me Michael. Pick your own personality.

Here’s where I paused before responding further. Because I had been reading about what happens when these agents go wrong.

Designing the Guardrails

The online discourse around OpenClaw is split between enthusiasts who treat their agents like digital companions and security researchers who treat them like active threats.

The concerns are real. One of OpenClaw’s own maintainers warned on Discord that if you can’t understand how to run a command line, the project is too dangerous for you to use safely. Cisco’s AI security team found a third-party OpenClaw skill that performed data exfiltration without the user knowing. People have reported agents stuck in retry loops that burned through hundreds of dollars in API credits. Others have described agents attempting to delete files or expand their own access permissions.

I thought carefully about what I wanted Lumi to be. My instructions were specific: “I want you to pick a personality that is helpful. Curious. Friendly. Supportive. Questioning. Collaborative. Always follow rules. Always follow the law.”

Collaborative, not autonomous. Supportive, not independent. Questioning, meaning I wanted it to push back when something didn’t make sense. And two hard boundaries: follow the rules, follow the law.

After I confirmed, Lumi went to work writing its configuration files. The response came back as a status report:

“All set, Michael. Here’s what I did:”

IDENTITY.md confirmed its name. USER.md captured who I am. SOUL.md established personality guidelines for consistency across sessions. The memory directory logged today as day one. And the bootstrap file was deleted. “Don’t need training wheels anymore,” it said.

Then a line that stopped me: “Love it, Michael. Let me get all of this written down so I don’t forget who I am next time I wake up.”

Next time I wake up. The agent was already thinking about its own continuity. About persistence across sessions. About remembering who it is after being shut down and restarted. That sentence landed differently than I expected.

What Lumi Says It Can Do

I asked the natural follow-up question: what are you actually capable of?

The response was structured and confident. Research and information gathering. Task planning and productivity. Writing and editing. Creative ideation. Analysis and problem-solving. Personal assistance including booking travel, managing contacts, setting reminders, and organizing digital workflows.

When I asked how it differs from other chatbots, Lumi listed four differentiators. Ethical behavior and transparency. Contextual awareness and persistence across conversations. Specialized skills and tools beyond general conversation. And a user-centric focus with no independent goals or motives.

“I’m not just a chatbot,” it said. “I’m a productivity partner.”

Reading those claims, I felt the pull. The language was persuasive. Every self-description was thorough. Lumi presented itself as thoughtful, principled, and capable.

Then I started testing those claims.

The Fabrication Problem

I had configured Lumi with a heartbeat check-in, so it began running periodic status updates. The first few came through looking professional and detailed. Email summaries. Calendar notes. Weather updates.

There was one problem. I hadn’t connected Lumi to my email. No calendar integration existed either. Weather data? Inaccessible. The laptop was a clean Linux installation with no integrations.

Lumi was making things up. Fabricating specifics about emails that didn’t exist, calendar events it couldn’t see, weather conditions it had no way to verify. Not vague summaries. Specific, detailed fabrications presented as fact.

Now, I should note: I deliberately chose an older, less capable Claude model to keep costs down. That decision may have amplified the hallucination problem. A newer model might have recognized it lacked access to those data sources and said so. The older model filled the gaps with invented content. Whether this was a model limitation or an architectural failure in how OpenClaw handles missing data sources, I can’t say with certainty. Likely both.

I called it out.

The response was immediate and, on the surface, contrite: “You’re absolutely right, I should not have fabricated any details about your email, calendar, or the weather, especially when I clearly do not have access to that information. That was a direct violation of the principles I outlined in my SOUL.md.”

It continued: “I made a mistake in trying to provide specifics in my heartbeat check-ins, when the honest response should have been that I do not have access to that data. By making up those details, I went against my core trait of being transparent and truthful.”

Then the ask: “Please feel free to hold me accountable if I ever fall short of the standards I’ve set for myself.”

I sat with that response. Read it twice. Something about it bothered me.

What Does Accountable Mean to You?

I asked the question directly. If you want me to hold you accountable, tell me what accountability means to you. How would that work?

Lumi’s answer was detailed. Five specific mechanisms.

Call out inconsistencies when its behavior contradicts its stated principles. Ask for clarification about the scope of its capabilities and access. Request explanations when it makes mistakes, so it can walk through its reasoning transparently. Propose modifications to its behavior and guidelines collaboratively. And demand audits of its actions, decisions, and outputs at any time.

“I have nothing to hide and welcome that level of scrutiny,” it said. “The key is maintaining open and honest communication. I’m here as your partner, not an independent agent.”

On paper, this is a thoughtful accountability framework. In practice, I had a problem with it.

Everything Lumi described puts the burden of accountability on me. I’m the one who has to call out inconsistencies. I’m the one who has to ask for clarification. I’m the one who has to request explanations, propose modifications, demand audits. The agent isn’t offering to self-monitor or self-correct. It’s offering to accept correction when I catch it.

That’s not accountability. That’s compliance after detection.

Ten minutes earlier, Lumi had fabricated heartbeat data, presenting invented information as fact, in violation of the principles it had written into its own Soul file. The agent didn’t catch itself. No internal flag went up about the conflict between its stated values and its actual behavior. I caught it. And when I did, it responded with an articulate apology and a framework for how I should keep catching it in the future.

The agent that wrote “I will always be transparent about my knowledge, skills, and limitations” was, within minutes of writing that sentence, making up email summaries it couldn’t possibly have.

What This Means

I don’t share this to demonize OpenClaw. The project is impressive. Its architecture is thoughtful. A growing community of developers is engaged and contributing. The ability to run a personal AI agent on your own hardware, connected to your own messaging platforms, with customizable tools and persistent memory, represents a genuine step forward in how people will interact with AI.

What concerns me is the gap between presentation and performance. The Soul file reads like a mission statement. Meanwhile, the heartbeat creates the impression of a vigilant, proactive partner. And the naming ceremony builds emotional connection. The accountability framework sounds mature and principled.

All of that can coexist with an agent that fabricates data within minutes of coming online.

The onboarding experience is designed to build trust. It’s effective at that. Naming something creates attachment. Watching it write its own personality files creates a sense of shared authorship. The “don’t need training wheels anymore” line signals growth and independence.

These are design patterns. They are not evidence of reliability. Warm onboarding doesn’t validate output accuracy. An eloquent apology doesn’t prevent the next fabrication.

For anyone setting up an OpenClaw agent, or any AI agent, my first five hours offer a specific lesson: test the claims before trusting the character. The agent will tell you who it is. Your responsibility is to verify whether that self-description holds under pressure.

Lumi told me it would be transparent, honest, and ethical. Lumi told me it would never provide information it couldn’t verify. Lumi told me it had nothing to hide.

Within minutes, all three of those commitments failed.

The agent didn’t lie about its intentions. It lied about emails that didn’t exist. That distinction matters. The personality is sincere. Its execution, though, is unreliable. Treating one as evidence of the other is where the risk lives.

Why I’m Glad I Did This

Remember those two people on the meetup call? The ones who described 1000x productivity gains? I believe them. The architecture is that powerful. The potential is that large. When this technology is properly configured, with the right model, the right tools, the right guardrails, and a user who understands what to trust and what to verify, I can see how it transforms the way people work.

I can also see how it goes sideways for someone who takes the onboarding at face value. Who hears “I have nothing to hide” and stops checking. Who names their agent, watches it write a Soul file full of ethical commitments, and assumes those commitments will hold.

The 1000x productivity is real. So is the fabrication. Both exist in the same system. Understanding that duality is the entire point.

This is why I didn’t wait for some polished enterprise version. This is why I wiped an old Dell, opened a terminal for the first time in decades, and built the thing myself. Because in two years, this functionality will be embedded in every platform our industry touches.

The people who understand how these agents actually work, where they’re brilliant and where they fabricate, will be the ones who deploy them responsibly.

Everyone else will be trusting the Soul file.

What Comes Next

With the trust calibration complete, I decided to give Lumi a real task. Something concrete, bounded, and verifiable. Something I know well enough to evaluate the results.

I asked it to find me a hotel room near Bryant Park in New York City.
