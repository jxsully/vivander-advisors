The Shoemaker Had Elves. What If Your Business Did Too?

Michael J. Goldrich · November 2, 2026

What would you try to build if your work could keep moving after you ran out of energy?

It is 11 p.m. You have been working all day, and you are not even halfway through. The proposal still needs attention. Your website is out of date. There is a new service you want to develop, assuming you ever find the time to think about it. Your to-do list is longer than your arm, and at this point you are not sure which item to tackle next.

So you do something familiar. Answer one more email. Move a few things to tomorrow. Close the laptop knowing that tomorrow already has more work than it can hold.

If you run a business on your own, you probably do not need me to explain that feeling. What interests me is how much of the business stays trapped there, in the gap between what you can imagine and what you have the capacity to do.

That is why AI agents make me think of a shoemaker.

The shoemaker had help he could not explain

In The Elves and the Shoemaker, a struggling craftsman cuts his leather, leaves it on the workbench and goes to bed. In the morning, someone has turned it into beautifully finished shoes. He has no idea who did it. But there they are, ready to sell.

As a child, you wonder about the elves. As someone running a business, you might find yourself thinking about the workbench. Imagine leaving an unfinished task there and returning to discover that it had moved forward without you.

The shoemaker’s skill has not changed overnight. Neither has the length of his day. What changes is the amount of work his little workshop can produce.

AI agents offer a version of that possibility to solopreneurs. Research can be gathered while you concentrate on a client. Notes can become a proposal draft. A tool you have been sketching in a notebook can begin to take shape as a prototype. You have something to inspect, rather than another idea you never got around to starting.

What can AI agents do for a solopreneur?

An AI agent uses an AI model and connected tools to work through steps toward a goal. With the right access, it can research, draft material or write and test code. You give it an assignment rather than personally directing every move.

Anthropic’s explanation of agent systems distinguishes between predefined workflows and agents that decide how to proceed as they work. Some of the work that used to stop when your attention moved elsewhere can now continue.

An agent can also misunderstand an assignment or get stuck. Overnight work requires a setup that can keep running: a local agent may stop when your laptop sleeps, while a cloud service may support background work. Either way, completion is never guaranteed.

Still, instead of facing a blank proposal tomorrow, you could have a draft with the missing information clearly marked. When you are already stretched, having somewhere to start matters.

The mysterious part makes you want to try again

Most people cannot explain exactly how an AI system turns an instruction into a piece of work. You ask it to investigate something, organize the findings and create a draft. Then you watch the result appear and wonder how far you could take it.

I think that sense of curiosity is important. There is a particular moment when a tool does something you had assumed was beyond your reach. You start looking at your own work differently. What about that idea? Could it help with this too?

The shoemaker had to wait for his helpers to appear. We can open a tool and ask. That is remarkable, even when the result needs correction.

The people who begin to benefit are willing to have that slightly awkward first encounter. They try a real task, discover that their instructions were too vague, clarify what they meant and try again. Gradually, they learn which assignments deserve the agent’s help and which are quicker to handle themselves.

Reading another article about AI will only take you so far. Eventually, one of the ideas in your notebook has to become an experiment.

How one person can deliver more like a larger business

Imagine a one-person consultancy advising independent hotels. Clients pay for the advisor’s experience and judgment, but a surprising amount of the week goes into preparing research, proposals, presentations and follow-up material. Developing a new service keeps getting pushed aside because serving today’s clients comes first.

Now give that advisor an agent to gather public information for a briefing, with links to the original sources. Another assignment turns approved workshop notes into draft exercises. Later, a coding agent helps build a small assessment tool that clients could try.

How Anthropic teams use Claude Code describes development and prototyping work of this kind. The distance between having an idea for a tool and having something to test is getting shorter.

But look at what has changed for our imagined advisor. The business now has a way to prepare more thoroughly and explore an idea that previously had no room in the calendar. The advisor still decides what matters, checks the evidence and owns the client relationship. There is simply more help available to turn that judgment into something useful.

That is the possibility I find compelling for solopreneurs. A one-person business can take on a broader range of work without requiring its owner to perform every supporting task. Whether that produces more revenue, better service or a less exhausting week depends on how the capacity is used.

Start with one thing you keep postponing

You do not need a collection of agents on your first evening. Choose one task that keeps slipping down the list, where a rough result would help and an error would be easy to catch. Public research or a first draft from non-sensitive notes can be a good place to start.

Give the agent the context you would give a capable person helping for the first time. Explain who the work is for, provide the material it should use and describe the result you want. For a proposal, you might add:

Flag missing information rather than inventing facts, prices or commitments. Save the draft for my review. Do not send or publish anything.

Stay close to the first attempt. Then ask whether it helped enough to justify the time you spent briefing and reviewing. That habit of trying real work and judging the result is central to Vivander’s practical AI training. You learn where the help is worth having.

You still own the workshop

The elves produced perfect shoes. Your agent will sometimes produce a confident mistake. Protect client information, check its work and keep spending and external actions behind approval controls. Tools and supervision both cost something; the help has to earn its place.

There is a detail in the fairy tale worth remembering: the shoemaker cut the leather before he went to bed. He left something the elves could work with. A clear brief and good source material are our version of that preparation.

You may still finish the day tired. The list may still be too long. But one of the things on it could be moving forward, ready for you to assess when you return.

Look at the idea you have carried from one week’s list to the next. If you had a little help in the workshop, what would you finally try?
