Is AI Recommending Your Hotel? The Screenshot Needs a Scorecard

Michael J. Goldrich · October 27, 2026

Your hotel appears in an AI answer. Someone takes a screenshot. It lands in the marketing team’s chat with an enthusiastic message: “We’re showing up!”

A perfectly reasonable moment to enjoy.

Before that screenshot reaches the owner’s report, ask one more question: what, exactly, did the answer do?

It might have named the hotel. It might have cited a page about the hotel. It might have recommended the hotel for the wrong guest, on the strength of an amenity that closed last year.

Those outcomes deserve different conversations.

To measure hotel AI visibility, track whether AI identifies the correct property, cites relevant evidence, recommends it for a suitable guest need, and provides an accurate next step. Measure actual guest choices separately. A test answer cannot tell you whether anyone booked.

What is the difference between mentioned, cited, recommended and chosen?

These four words make a useful reporting vocabulary, provided everyone agrees on their meaning.

Mentioned: The answer names your hotel. Record the context. Appearing in a warning about expensive parking still counts as a mention, but it hardly deserves confetti.

Cited: The answer links to a source that supports a statement. Identify whether that source is your website, a booking partner or another publisher. Check the passage itself. A link beside a claim does not establish that the page supports it.

Recommended: The answer presents your hotel as an option for the traveler’s stated needs. Assess the reasoning and constraints. A property outside the requested budget can be recommended and still be a poor answer.

Chosen: A real traveler selects the hotel. Be explicit about the evidence: a saved shortlist, a booking or a completed stay. Those are different events. An assistant saying “I would choose this hotel” remains a recommendation unless a real selection follows.

Do not force these into a neat funnel. A hotel can be recommended without its own website being cited. A page can be cited without the property being recommended. A traveler can discover the hotel in AI and book through a different channel.

How do you test recommendations without grading your own homework?

Write the scoring rules before you run the questions.

Start with commercial situations the hotel actually serves. For example, an airport property might test early departures, overnight arrivals and short stays between flights. A resort might test multigenerational trips, specific activities and seasonal availability.

Keep discovery and brand knowledge separate. “Tell me about Hotel Example” supplies the answer’s subject. “Where should I stay before a 6 a.m. flight?” asks the system to find one.

For each response, record five judgments:

  1. Identity: Is this the correct property and location?
  2. Inclusion: Is it merely named, offered as an option or explicitly preferred?
  3. Fit: Does the recommendation satisfy the traveler’s stated constraints?
  4. Evidence: Are material claims supported by current, relevant sources?
  5. Action: Does the next step lead to the correct property, with important limitations clear?

Allow “unknown” when a fact cannot be verified. Otherwise the scorecard will quietly turn missing evidence into confidence.

Have a second person review a sample, especially borderline recommendations. If marketing calls an answer excellent while reservations sees an impossible promise, that disagreement is a finding worth investigating.

What does a defensible visibility report look like?

A report should let someone repeat the same test protocol, even though the answers may differ. Preserve the prompt, date, platform, available model or mode, browsing or search settings, language, personalization state, account and location context, answer and sources. Repeat important questions in fresh conversations. Keep a stable core for comparisons and a separate group for new questions. This approach builds on the distinction between sampled visibility and commercial outcomes in The Guest Arrives With a Shortlist.

Report actual counts beside percentages. Consider this fictional result:

  • 105 test attempts made: 100 valid responses collected and five technical failures.
  • Correct property mentioned in 42.
  • Property recommended in 28.
  • Recommendation met all verified constraints in 19.
  • Six of the other nine recommendations contained a material factual error. The remaining three had an unresolved fit question.

The 28 recommendations represent 28% of those test responses. They do not establish that 28% of travelers see the hotel. These groups do not overlap: 19 verified fits, six recommendations with material errors and three with unresolved fit questions make up the 28 recommendations.

Use the 100 valid responses as the denominator for response-level rates, and report the five technical failures separately against all 105 attempts. Keep that denominator policy consistent between reporting periods. An answer that declines to recommend any hotel is a valid response and should stay in the results.

Keep platform results separate before presenting an aggregate. Combining unequal numbers of tests can make performance appear to improve simply because you ran more questions on a favorable platform. Weighting, if used, needs an explicit business rationale. It should never be presented as measured traveler demand without evidence.

How do you connect AI visibility to bookings?

Use three views together: sampled answers, observed traffic and business outcomes.

Google’s dedicated generative-AI performance reports show impressions of site URLs in its AI experiences, with breakdowns including pages and countries. That is useful platform evidence, not a count of hotel recommendations or reservations. Google’s reporting announcement explains the scope.

On your own site, examine identifiable AI referrals and their subsequent booking activity where measurement is supported and consent permits it. Look at cancellations and contribution, too. A reservation that never becomes a stay has different economics.

An optional guest discovery question can add context. Ask neutrally and allow multiple answers. Treat recollection as self-reported evidence, not proof of causation.

For an incremental-booking claim, design a credible comparison that accounts for rates, inventory, events, seasonality and other marketing changes. A better visibility score followed by a stronger month is an association worth examining. It does not isolate what caused the improvement.

What should the hotel fix first?

Prioritize the errors most likely to create a bad guest decision: the wrong location, misleading accessibility information, unavailable facilities, incorrect fees or unsupported guarantees.

Then address missing facts that prevent a good-fit traveler from judging the property. Have the operational owner verify the content before marketing publishes it.

Google’s current optimization guidance continues to emphasize useful original content and established SEO practices. Building hundreds of nearly identical pages for hypothetical prompts creates work without establishing that the hotel deserves a recommendation.

Bring one answer to your next commercial meeting. Ask the marketing, reservations and operations leads to score it independently.

If they disagree, you have found something more useful than a flattering screenshot: a specific decision about what to correct.

Common questions

Is a citation better than a mention?

It provides a source to inspect, but it is not automatically a stronger commercial outcome. Evaluate what the answer says, whether the source supports it and whether the hotel fits the guest’s needs.

How often should a hotel check AI recommendations?

Use a consistent schedule that matches the property’s risk and pace of change, with extra checks after important policy or service changes. Increase testing when results are unstable. No universal frequency makes a sample representative of all travelers.

Can an AI visibility score prove booking ROI?

No. It describes performance within a defined test. Booking ROI requires costs, financial outcomes and credible evidence about what the visibility work changed.
