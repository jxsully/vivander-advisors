AI Visual Content Discovery: The Shift to Images

Michael J. Goldrich · January 21, 2026

Why Google Says Your Photos Speak Louder Than Your Meta Tags

The way travelers find their next hotel is undergoing a fundamental transformation. AI systems are learning to read images and understand photos the way humans do. A swim-up bar that appears in your property’s video can now surface your hotel in a traveler’s search, even if those words never appear in your website copy. This shift from text-based optimization to visual content discovery represents one of the most significant changes in hospitality marketing since the rise of online travel agencies.

At the Skift Megatrends event in London on January 20, 2026, Jay Chauhan, Google’s industry head for travel, outlined how AI is reshaping travel discovery. His message to hospitality brands was direct: stop obsessing over generic keywords and start thinking about how AI interprets your content, from photos and videos to guest reviews and user intent.

This shift matters far beyond marketing departments. It touches every team responsible for creating, curating, and publishing content about a property. And it raises strategic questions that reach the executive suite: How do we maintain visibility when the rules of discovery are being rewritten?

What Is Changing

The Decline of Generic Keywords

Traditional search engine optimization taught hospitality marketers to compete for broad terms. “All-inclusive holiday.” “Beachfront resort.” “Luxury hotel.” These generic phrases drove traffic for years.

That era is ending.

Google reports double-digit increases in searches for specific hotel names while clicks on broad category terms continue to decline. Travelers are arriving at Google search with their research already complete. They have spent time with conversational AI assistants asking complex, layered questions about their ideal trip. By the time they reach a search engine, they know what they want. They type a specific property name and book.

The research phase has moved upstream. It now happens in conversations with ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, and other large language models. Google’s data shows that 94% of people who use LLMs also continue to use traditional search. However, they use each platform differently. Conversational AI handles exploration. Search engines handle verification and booking.

This split creates a new challenge for hospitality brands. Winning the booking requires being present in both environments. Appearing in the conversational exploration phase requires a different approach than ranking for keywords.

AI Systems That Read Visual Content

Google’s AI capabilities now include something remarkable: the ability to identify amenities from photos and videos. Chauhan described these as “superpowers” that allow the search engine to surface properties based on visual evidence, even when text descriptions omit relevant details.

Consider the implications. A boutique hotel in Lisbon has a stunning rooftop terrace with city views. The marketing copy emphasizes history, design, and location. The word “rooftop” appears nowhere on the website. Traditional SEO would never connect this property to a traveler searching for rooftop experiences.

AI visual analysis changes this equation. If that rooftop appears in photos or video content, the AI can recognize it. The property can now surface for queries it never explicitly optimized for. The visual evidence becomes discoverable.

This capability inverts the traditional relationship between content creation and optimization. In the keyword era, brands decided which terms to target and then created content to match. In the visual discovery era, brands create authentic content and AI determines what queries it answers.

The Marriage of Structured and Unstructured Data

Travel marketing has long relied on structured data: star ratings, location coordinates, amenity checkboxes, price ranges. These machine-readable fields help search engines categorize and compare properties. They remain essential.

What has changed is the growing importance of unstructured data, particularly guest reviews. A pricing feed can transmit rates. An amenity list can indicate pool availability. Neither can convey that a property feels romantic.

AI systems can infer emotional qualities from review language. “Perfect anniversary trip.” “Intimate dining experience.” “Secluded and peaceful.” These phrases, scattered across hundreds of reviews, create a composite picture of guest sentiment. AI can read that picture and match it to traveler intent.

A guest searching for “romantic getaway in Tuscany” is expressing an emotional need, different from a practical need. The traveler wants a feeling, not a room category. Properties that generate reviews containing emotional language gain visibility for emotional queries. Properties that generate reviews focused on operational details (clean rooms, fast check-in) may struggle to surface for experiential searches.

The Boundless Curiosity of Travelers

Google processes approximately five trillion searches per year. Fifteen percent of those queries are entirely new. They have never been asked before in that exact form.

This statistic reveals something important about how people use search. Travelers arrive with questions that existing content may never have anticipated. “What’s the best hiking near Sedona for someone recovering from knee surgery?” “Family-friendly hotels in Rome with interconnecting rooms and a kids’ menu that serves vegetables?”

AI-powered search is designed to answer novel questions by synthesizing information from multiple sources. The system does not require an exact keyword match. It requires relevant, well-structured content that AI can understand and recombine.

For hospitality brands, this creates an opportunity. Properties that create comprehensive, detailed content give AI systems more material to work with. The AI can extract relevant pieces and assemble them into answers for questions the property never anticipated.

Why This Matters

The Visibility Power Shift

Discovery is becoming a two-stage process. Travelers explore options in conversational AI environments. They verify and book through traditional search engines and trusted websites. Brands that appear in both stages capture bookings. Brands visible in only one stage risk losing travelers to competitors who show up earlier in the journey.

The economic implications are significant. If a property invests heavily in Google Ads but remains invisible to conversational AI, it competes for travelers who have already formed preferences during their AI exploration. Winning a click from a decided traveler costs more than earning consideration from an undecided one.

Visibility in AI-mediated contexts functions as a competitive moat. Properties that understand how to appear in conversational recommendations gain advantage. Properties that rely solely on traditional search tactics will find their competitive position eroding.

The Content Value Reassessment

Photos, videos, and guest reviews have always mattered to hospitality marketing. What has changed is their role in discovery, not persuasion.

In the traditional model, a traveler found a property through search or OTA listings, then viewed photos to decide whether to book. Visual content served as closing material. It convinced travelers who had already arrived.

In the AI visual discovery model, photos and videos serve as opening material. They determine whether the property surfaces at all. A hotel with comprehensive video content showing its spa, restaurant, rooftop, and guest rooms provides AI systems with rich material to index and match. A hotel with a few professional photos of the lobby provides less.

This shift has resource implications. The investment case for content creation strengthens when that content drives discovery, rather than conversion alone.

The Review Sentiment Opportunity

Guest reviews have long influenced booking decisions through star ratings and social proof. Their new function as source material for AI responses adds another dimension.

Properties that systematically generate reviews containing specific, emotional, and descriptive language give AI systems more to work with. A review stating “The staff remembered our anniversary and left champagne in the room” provides information that no structured data field can capture.

Encouraging guests to share detailed stories, rather than generic praise, becomes a strategic priority. The operational challenge lies in prompting specificity without appearing to script reviews. The opportunity lies in the compounding returns from reviews that teach AI systems what makes a property distinctive.

What This Means for Work

Content Teams: From Writers to Visual Curators

Marketing teams in hospitality have traditionally focused on copy: website text, email campaigns, social captions, advertising headlines. Visual content existed as support material, selected from existing photography or commissioned periodically.

The AI visual discovery environment elevates visual curation to a primary responsibility. Content teams must now think systematically about what their visual library teaches AI systems about the property. Gaps in visual coverage translate to gaps in discoverability.

This requires new workflows:

  • Auditing existing photo and video libraries for completeness
  • Identifying amenities and experiences that lack visual documentation
  • Coordinating with operations to capture authentic guest moments
  • Evaluating content through the lens of AI interpretation, not human persuasion
  • Building relationships with user-generated content creators who produce discoverable material

The skills required for this work differ from traditional copywriting. Visual storytelling, content cataloging, and AI comprehension replace SEO keyword research and A/B headline testing.

Operations Teams: Data Providers for Discovery

Property operations generate signals that AI systems increasingly consume. Guest reviews reflect operational quality. Social media posts capture real experiences. Photo opportunities exist throughout the guest journey.

Operations teams become data providers in this environment. The concierge who creates a memorable experience generates a review. The housekeeping team that enables a special room setup creates a shareable moment. The restaurant manager who accommodates a dietary request earns a mention in a guest story.

This reframing does not require operations to “do marketing.” It requires recognition that operational excellence now has discoverability consequences. The way guests describe their experience, in reviews, posts, and conversations, shapes how AI systems categorize and recommend the property.

Revenue Teams: Managing the Dual Funnel

Revenue management has traditionally focused on optimizing the path from search impression to booking confirmation. Rate optimization, inventory distribution, and promotional timing all target travelers who have already expressed intent.

The dual-funnel reality introduces a new challenge: managing visibility in the conversational exploration phase that precedes search intent. Revenue teams cannot directly purchase placement in ChatGPT’s recommendations the way they can buy Google Ads. They must influence upstream visibility through content strategy and brand positioning.

This creates tension. Promotional rates and discounted packages may drive bookings but could generate reviews focused on price rather than experience. Operational investments that improve guest experience may not show immediate revenue impact but could generate reviews that improve AI visibility for high-intent queries.

Revenue strategy must account for both funnels. Short-term conversion optimization and long-term discoverability investment require different metrics and different time horizons.

What This Means for Leadership

Visibility as Strategic Priority

Visibility in AI-mediated discovery environments becomes a board-level concern. The question “How do travelers find us?” now has multiple answers that require coordinated investment across marketing, content, operations, and technology.

Leadership teams must understand the dual-funnel model and allocate resources accordingly. Over-investment in traditional search marketing while neglecting conversational AI visibility creates future competitive exposure. Under-investment in booking conversion while chasing AI visibility sacrifices current revenue for uncertain future returns.

The strategic challenge lies in balancing both. Properties need presence in both environments. Neither alone suffices.

Technology Infrastructure Decisions

AI systems consume content differently than human visitors. Machine-readable formats, structured data schemas, and content organization for AI extraction require technology investments that may not have existed in current roadmaps.

Leadership decisions about content management systems, digital asset management, and data architecture now carry visibility consequences. A platform that makes content easy for humans to browse but difficult for AI to parse creates a discoverability handicap.

These decisions often fall between traditional functional boundaries. Marketing owns content strategy. Technology owns platform architecture. Neither alone can solve the AI visibility challenge. Leadership must create cross-functional accountability.

Talent and Capability Building

The skills required for AI-era visibility differ from traditional hospitality marketing competencies. Visual content strategy, AI prompt comprehension, structured data management, and review sentiment analysis represent emerging capability requirements.

Leadership faces choices about building versus buying these capabilities. Training existing teams requires investment in education and time for skill development. Hiring specialists requires competing for scarce talent in a market where AI skills command premiums.

The organizations that move earliest in building these capabilities will compound their advantage. AI systems learn from content. Properties that create the right content first establish positions that become harder for competitors to displace.

Why Literacy and Visibility Matter

Literacy Enables Appropriate Response

Understanding how AI systems discover and recommend travel options allows organizations to respond appropriately. Without this understanding, teams make decisions based on outdated mental models.

A marketing director who believes visibility still depends primarily on keyword rankings will allocate resources accordingly. That allocation may neglect the visual content, guest review generation, and structured data investments that increasingly drive discoverability.

Literacy does not mean every team member must understand AI architectures. It means decision-makers grasp the changing mechanics of discovery well enough to evaluate investment tradeoffs. It means content creators understand what AI systems can and cannot interpret from their work. It means operations teams recognize their role in generating discoverable guest experiences.

Visibility Functions as Competitive Power

In markets where travelers explore options through conversational AI before committing to search, visibility in those conversations determines consideration. Properties that appear in AI recommendations earn a position in the traveler’s mental shortlist. Properties absent from those conversations compete for travelers who have already formed preferences.

This dynamic compounds over time. AI systems learn from user behavior. Properties that earn clicks from AI recommendations signal relevance. That relevance improves future recommendation frequency. Properties absent from initial recommendations lose the opportunity to generate relevance signals.

Early visibility creates lasting advantage. Late recognition creates lasting disadvantage. The window between these outcomes may be shorter than most hospitality organizations expect.

Framework for Action

Three Investments for AI Discovery Readiness

Visual Content Audit and Expansion

Map existing photo and video content against the full range of property amenities, experiences, and emotional qualities. Identify gaps where significant features lack visual documentation. Prioritize content creation based on traveler query patterns and competitive differentiation opportunities.

Success indicator: Every distinctive property feature appears in multiple visual formats (photo, video, user-generated content) with metadata that AI systems can interpret.

Review Generation Strategy

Design guest communication touchpoints that encourage detailed, specific, emotionally descriptive reviews. Identify operational moments that generate memorable experiences worth describing. Track review sentiment for language that AI systems associate with high-value travel queries (romantic, family-friendly, business-appropriate, adventure-ready).

Success indicator: Review portfolio contains rich, specific language across multiple experience categories, providing AI systems with material to match diverse traveler intents.

Structured Data Foundation

Ensure property information exists in formats AI systems can consume. Schema markup, accurate location data, complete amenity listings, and consistent naming conventions across platforms create the foundation for AI visibility. Audit existing structured data for gaps and inconsistencies.

Success indicator: Property information appears consistently across all platforms AI systems crawl, with complete structured data markup that enables accurate categorization.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is AI visual content discovery?

AI visual content discovery is the ability of search engines and AI systems to understand and index visual content like photos and videos, surfacing relevant results even when text descriptions lack specific keywords.

Google’s AI systems can now identify amenities and features from visual content. A hotel with a swim-up bar visible in photos can appear in searches for that feature even if the website copy never mentions it. This represents a fundamental shift from text-based keyword matching to content comprehension that more closely resembles human understanding. Properties with comprehensive visual documentation gain visibility advantages over those relying primarily on written descriptions.

2. Why are generic keywords becoming less effective for hotels?

Generic keywords face declining effectiveness because travelers now conduct exploratory research with conversational AI before using search engines, arriving at Google with specific property names rather than broad category searches.

The research that once happened in Google search now happens in ChatGPT, Gemini, and similar platforms. Travelers ask these AI assistants layered questions about their ideal trip. By the time they reach a search engine, they often already know which property they want. They search for the specific hotel name to verify information and complete booking. Generic keyword rankings matter less when travelers skip the discovery phase in traditional search.

3. How do guest reviews affect AI visibility?

Guest reviews provide AI systems with unstructured data that conveys emotional and experiential qualities no amenity checklist can capture, influencing which properties surface for sentiment-based traveler queries.

A pricing feed tells AI systems about rates. An amenity list indicates pool availability. Neither conveys that a property feels romantic, adventurous, or family-friendly. AI systems infer these qualities from review language. Properties generating reviews with specific, emotional, descriptive content give AI more material to match against traveler intent. “Perfect anniversary trip” and “kids loved the pool” teach AI systems different things about the property.

4. What should hotels do about AI-powered search changes?

Hotels should audit visual content completeness, ensure comprehensive structured data, encourage detailed guest reviews, and develop content strategies that address both traditional search and conversational AI discovery.

Google’s advice through Jay Chauhan at the Skift Megatrends event emphasized pragmatic action over perfect strategy. The landscape continues evolving. Organizations that experiment and iterate will learn faster than those waiting for clarity. Start with a visual content audit identifying undocumented amenities. Implement structured data markup. Design review solicitation approaches that encourage specificity. Monitor how AI systems present your property in test queries.

5. What does this mean for hospitality marketing teams?

Marketing teams must expand beyond copywriting and keyword optimization to become visual content curators who understand how AI systems interpret and surface property information.

Traditional hospitality marketing skills centered on persuasive writing, campaign management, and keyword research. AI visual discovery adds new requirements: visual storytelling, content cataloging, metadata management, and AI system comprehension. The role evolves from creating content that persuades arrived visitors to creating content that enables AI systems to recommend the property during traveler exploration phases.

6. How important is the difference between structured and unstructured data?

Both data types serve essential but different functions: structured data enables categorization and comparison while unstructured data like reviews conveys nuanced qualities that determine emotional fit for traveler intent.

Structured data (star ratings, location, amenity checkboxes, price ranges) helps AI systems sort and filter properties. Unstructured data (reviews, social posts, descriptive content) helps AI systems understand experiential qualities. A property can have perfect structured data but weak unstructured data, appearing in filtered searches but missing from experiential queries like “romantic weekend escape.” Both investments matter.

7. Why does Google say 94% of LLM users also use traditional search?

This statistic reveals that conversational AI and traditional search serve different purposes in the travel journey: AI handles exploration and planning while search engines handle verification and booking conversion.

Travelers use conversational AI to work through complex, multi-layered questions about their ideal trip. These queries are often two to three times longer than traditional search queries. Once travelers narrow their options, they return to familiar search interfaces and trusted websites to verify information and complete bookings. Hospitality brands need visibility in both environments to capture the full traveler journey.

8. What role do operations teams play in AI visibility?

Operations teams generate the experiences that become guest reviews, photos, and stories, the raw material AI systems use to understand and recommend properties to travelers.

The concierge who creates a memorable moment generates a review. The housekeeping team enabling a special setup creates a shareable photo. The restaurant accommodating dietary needs earns a mention in a guest story. Operations does not need to “do marketing.” Operations needs to recognize that exceptional experiences now have discoverability consequences. How guests describe what happened shapes how AI systems categorize the property.

9. What should executives understand about AI discovery?

Executives must recognize that visibility in AI-mediated discovery is becoming a strategic priority requiring coordinated investment across marketing, content, operations, and technology functions.

This is not a marketing department issue alone. AI visibility depends on visual content (marketing), guest experience (operations), data architecture (technology), and review sentiment (cross-functional). Leadership must create accountability structures that bridge these boundaries. The dual-funnel reality (conversational AI for exploration, traditional search for booking) requires balanced investment in both environments.

10. How quickly is this change happening?

The transition is accelerating, with Google reporting immediate shifts in search behavior patterns, though the full impact will unfold over the coming years as AI capabilities continue expanding.

Jay Chauhan’s advice reflects current urgency: “Don’t let perfect get in the way of good. Launch and iterate.” Properties that wait for perfect AI strategies will fall behind competitors who experiment now. The compounding nature of AI visibility (early presence generates signals that reinforce future recommendations) means timing matters. Organizations that build AI discovery capabilities today position themselves for advantages that become harder for late movers to replicate.
