Travel in the Age of AI: McKinsey Shares Vision at HSMAI Curate

Michael J. Goldrich · November 18, 2024

Last week at HSMAI’s Curate event in Washington, DC, McKinsey partners Alex Gersovitz and Bryce Hall delivered a compelling presentation on AI’s role in travel and hospitality. Their session, “Travel in the Age of AI,” cut through typical tech hype to deliver practical insights for industry leaders.

Hall, a senior expert at McKinsey who advises organizations on digital and analytics transformations, opened with a calming perspective. “This is just one among many paradigm shifts – an important one, but not dissimilar to previous transitions the travel industry has navigated.” His publications in Harvard Business Review and MIT Sloan Management Review lent weight to this measured approach.

The Big Picture: $73 Billion Opportunity
McKinsey’s research projects AI will add $73 billion in value specifically to travel and hospitality. This comes from their broader study showing $4 trillion in potential global AI impact. While these numbers sound massive, Hall broke them down into digestible pieces.

Three Paths to AI Implementation:

  1. Off-the-shelf solutions: Ready-to-use tools like chatbots and content creation systems that need minimal setup
  2. Custom-trained solutions: Systems requiring company data and several months to implement
  3. Built-from-scratch systems: Full custom development, mainly for major chains

Hall shared a striking example of AI’s evolution in their own work. “Our developers write no new code without AI assistance now. That’s a dramatic shift from just three years ago.” But he emphasized this hasn’t replaced human expertise – it’s enhanced it.

Real World Success and Stumbles
The presentation included candid stories from early adopters. One hotel chain’s attempt to make their chatbot “friendly” backfired when it started calling guests “honey.” This led to an important discussion about maintaining appropriate brand voice while leveraging AI capabilities.

More successful was a sales team’s use of AI for customer research. The system sends sales staff detailed briefings five minutes before each meeting, including the customer’s history and likely priorities. “This isn’t revolutionary,” Hall noted, “sales teams have always done this research. But AI makes it instant and comprehensive.”

The Human Element Remains Critical
A key theme emerged around preserving hospitality’s human touch. The speakers emphasized that AI should handle routine tasks to free staff for meaningful guest interactions. “The technology supports but never supplants the human connection,” Gersovitz stressed.

Implementation Roadmap
The speakers outlined a practical approach:

  • Start with understanding your current operation
  • Identify specific pain points where AI could help
  • Begin with small, measurable pilot programs
  • Focus heavily on staff training and adoption
  • Scale only what proves valuable

Risks and Challenges
Using a vivid analogy, Hall compared current AI adoption to “opening Jurassic Park before installing the electric fences.” They stressed the need for careful testing, strong governance, and clear protocols for AI use.

The Path Forward
Both speakers emphasized that success with AI isn’t about technology – it’s about solving real business problems. They advised against rushing to implement AI everywhere, instead recommending a thoughtful approach that starts with clear business objectives.

The lasting message was optimistic but grounded: AI represents a massive opportunity for travel and hospitality, but capturing its value requires patience, strategy, and unwavering focus on enhancing rather than replacing the human elements that make hospitality special.
