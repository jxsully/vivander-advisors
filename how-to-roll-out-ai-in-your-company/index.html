Start here

Start with a recurring business problem.

Choose a task that matters, happens often and produces a result someone can check. Assign an owner, record the current process and test one focused workflow with a small group.

The short answer: agree the outcome, choose a pilot, prepare the inputs, train the people and compare the results with your baseline. Expand when the evidence supports it.

Software access is one part of the plan. Employees also need to know where AI belongs in their work, what a good result looks like and who can help when it falls short.

A rollout with clear responsibilities

  1. 01 Define the outcomeA business goal and a named workflow owner
  2. 02 Prepare the workflowInputs, tool, review standard and baseline
  3. 03 Run a focused pilotRepresentative tasks, including difficult cases
  4. 04 Equip the teamRole-based practice, feedback and support
  5. 05 Decide with evidenceExpand, revise or stop; then keep measuring

A plan that guides decisions

Make these six decisions before the pilot.

01

What needs to improve?

Describe the delay, quality gap or repetitive work. Write the outcome in business language, such as reducing the time needed to prepare a verified weekly brief.

02

Which task comes first?

Compare value, frequency, usable inputs and review effort. Start where the team can evaluate the output and learn quickly.

03

Who owns the result?

Name a sponsor and a workflow owner. Identify the users, reviewer, technology contact and person who handles exceptions.

04

What can AI use and do?

Choose an approved tool, suitable inputs and clear action boundaries. Decide which outputs need review before they are used.

05

How will people learn?

Train people on their next real task. Include managers and reviewers, practice time and a place to ask questions.

06

What earns expansion?

Agree the quality standard and measures before testing. Include total effort, repeat use, exceptions and maintenance.

A worked example

Turn an AI ambition into a testable pilot.

Too broad

“Use AI to improve sales.”

No defined output, reviewer or way to tell whether the change helped.

Ready to test
“Prepare a draft account brief from these approved sources. Link every factual claim to its source. Flag missing information. Give the brief to the account manager for review.”
Illustrative pilot: account-brief preparation. This is an example of planning a test, not a Vivander client result.

Complete several briefs with the existing process first. Record preparation time, checking time and the number of corrections. Then use the same type of accounts in the AI-assisted test.

Include a routine account, an account with little information and one with conflicting sources. The reviewer checks accuracy, relevance and whether the brief supports the next conversation.

Compare the complete process. A draft generated in seconds may still take too long to repair. The useful measure includes preparation, review, correction and ongoing upkeep.

Adoption is a team capability

Teach the workflow. Practice the judgment.

Show employees how to provide context, recognize a weak result, verify a claim and decide when AI should not be used. Give reviewers examples of acceptable and unacceptable outputs.

Hold a short follow-up after people have tried the workflow in normal work. Use their questions and corrections to improve the instructions and training.

Ask the team

  • Which step became easier?
  • Where did checking take longer?
  • What did the instructions leave unclear?
  • What would help employees use this workflow again?

Listen for changes in workload, quality and confidence. Login counts alone do not answer these questions.

The decision point

Scale a repeatable result.

Expand when the output meets the agreed standard, total effort is worthwhile and an owner can maintain the process. Add the next group in stages so new problems stay visible.

Expand when

  • Quality is consistent across representative tasks.
  • Users return to the workflow without constant rescue.
  • Review, support and exceptions have clear owners.

Revise when

  • Corrections consume the expected benefit.
  • The tool needs inputs the team cannot supply.
  • Employees avoid it because the workflow does not fit.

Review again when tools, source information or responsibilities change. Stopping a weak pilot can be a useful outcome when it prevents a larger investment in an unsuitable process.

An AI pilot launch checklist

  • One task, one defined output and a named owner
  • A recorded baseline and an agreed quality standard
  • Approved tools, inputs and action boundaries
  • Routine, incomplete and conflicting test cases
  • A reviewer and a route for exceptions
  • Practice time and follow-up support
  • A decision date with criteria to expand, revise or stop

Questions people ask

Does your organization need new software?

Begin with the tools your organization already approves. Add software when a specific workflow requires it, after considering setup, support, review and maintenance.

How long does an AI rollout take?

Timing depends on the workflow, data readiness, people and scope. Set a decision date for the first pilot and agree a realistic schedule before expanding. This guide is a planning sequence, not a promised delivery timetable.
