AI Booking Shift: What the Destination AI Forum Revealed About the Future

Michael J. Goldrich · March 15, 2026

Source: Destination AI Forum Virtual Session, “AI & The Next Booking Shift,” March 13, 2025. Session one panelists: Harriet Brown (Mobi), Michael Mahar (Wyndham Hotels and Resorts), Brad Brewer (Agentic Hospitality), Jason Cincotta (Kismet). Session two panelists: Andrew Beckmann (Stripe), Sanjay Vakil (DirectBooker), Greg Duff (Foster Garvey PC), Tyrone Millard (OpenAI), Ira Vouk (Hospitality 2.0). Session one moderated by Michael J. Goldrich (Vivander Advisors). Session two moderated by Ira Vouk. Event organized by Naz Aydin of Destination AI.

Something Real Is Happening With AI Bookings

On March 13, 2025, Destination AI hosted a virtual session with ten practitioners working at the edge of AI and hospitality. Four hundred people registered. Two panels ran back to back. The first explored how guests find hotels now and how they will book them soon. The second worked through the payment infrastructure, legal exposure, and privacy questions that agentic commerce is already raising. I moderated session one.

The conversations were grounded in the present. No one was speculating about a distant future. These were people describing what is already working, what is already breaking, and where the gaps are right now.

How Guests Find Hotels Now and How They Will Book Soon

–

The Conversation Has Replaced the Search Bar

The behavior change is the starting point, because everything in distribution follows from it.

Sanjay Vakil of DirectBooker described his own recent experience planning a trip to Berlin. Rather than opening a travel site and scrolling through hundreds of paginated listings, he described his constraints to an AI platform in plain language. He wanted proximity to a specific subway stop. He had a budget. He had preferences. The AI returned six hotels. Strong candidates. Relevant to what he actually asked.

The filtering that used to happen across twenty pages of results happened inside the conversation instead.

Andrew Beckmann of Stripe put it this way: the value goes beyond efficiency. It is wholeness. Hotel, meeting locations, restaurants, transit options all assembled within a single conversational thread. That integrated experience is what makes the behavior sticky for travelers who try it once.

Tyrone Millard of OpenAI described personalization at a granular level that traditional search has never delivered. His team uses an internal example I find myself returning to: finding a hotel with a curved shower rail. That specific, sensory preference has never been surfable through a keyword search. In a conversational AI environment, it is a straightforward request. The platform holds that preference in context and uses it to filter.

OpenAI recently crossed 900 million weekly active users. The growth rate internationally is striking. Germany, India, and the Asia-Pacific region are seeing AI adoption rates that rival or exceed what the United States has seen. Close to double digits of Germany’s entire population uses the tool every single day. This is mainstream behavior, happening at the center of the travel industry. It is the center of gravity for how a large and growing share of travelers will plan trips.

Three Scenarios Worth Understanding Clearly

“AI booking” means different things to different people. Ira Vouk named three distinct scenarios at the start of session two, and the distinction matters because each one requires a completely different organizational response.

Scenario One: AI-Assisted Discovery. A traveler uses ChatGPT or Gemini to research properties, receives a recommendation, and books through the hotel website or an OTA outside the chat window. This scenario is active today at meaningful scale.

Scenario Two: AI-Mediated Booking. An AI system embedded within a hotel’s own website or app manages the conversation and completes the transaction within that controlled environment. The hotel determines what the AI can say and do. Several forward-thinking properties are live with this now.

Scenario Three: Fully Automated Agent-to-Agent Commerce. No human triggers the booking. An AI agent sees a meeting appear on a calendar, identifies the need for accommodation, negotiates with a hotel-side AI agent, and delivers a confirmed reservation to an inbox. The traveler participates only by receiving the result. This is where the trajectory is pointing.

Each scenario requires different technical infrastructure, different data organization, and a different definition of what “direct booking” actually means. Many hotel organizations are building for scenario one while scenario three is already being designed for.

The Timeline: Thirty-Six Months from Discovery to Execution

We are roughly two and a half years into the AI era as a commercially relevant surface for hospitality. The mobile era took five years for hotels to meaningfully participate. Brad Brewer of Agentic Hospitality sees a thirty-six-month arc before agent-to-agent commerce becomes mainstream, and he broke it into three phases.

Today: discovery and research. Travelers use AI to learn, compare, and narrow options. The AI is a research partner. The human still makes the final call and completes the transaction manually.

Twelve to twenty-four months from now: execution begins. Travelers build itineraries inside AI interfaces. They compose carts. Transactions initiate from within AI surfaces rather than being completed on separate websites.

Thirty-six months out: agent-to-agent commerce for unmanaged business travel. A corporate traveler specifies the parameters. The AI broadcasts the need. The properties with the right infrastructure to receive, process, and respond win the booking. Properties without that infrastructure are invisible to the transaction.

Brewer compared where we are now to 2010 in the mobile era, before hotels understood that the phone was where guests wanted to engage. The brands that moved early built advantages that took years for competitors to close.

What Hotels Need to Have in Place

–

MCP Is the Infrastructure Question You Cannot Defer

Model Context Protocol came up in both panels, across multiple conversations, from multiple angles. That volume of attention is itself a signal.

Mike Mahar of Wyndham offered the clearest explanation. Think of MCP as a USB cable connecting hotel data directly to an AI model. When an LLM needs real-time inventory, current pricing, and specific room attributes, MCP is what provides that direct connection. Without it, the AI reaches for whatever web sources it can find.

Harriet Brown of Mobi made the business consequence concrete. Without an MCP solution in place, a query about a hotel surfaces third-party web results: OTA listings, review site data, potentially outdated descriptions. The AI frequently recommends booking through an OTA because that is the most structured and accessible data it can find. With an MCP solution active, the same query returns first-party data, real-time availability, accurate current pricing, and a direct booking link.

The Wyndham and Mobi case study showed this difference side by side. The output quality gap is substantial.

Many hotel executives are getting this wrong. An MCP solution demands more than a configuration task. It goes far beyond wrapping existing website APIs and calling it done. An MCP solution is an AI product in its own right. The quality of that product directly influences how much traffic an LLM routes toward it. LLMs evaluate the quality of what they can access. A weak implementation returns weak results and gets deprioritized.

Wyndham Connect with Canary, their LLM-supported guest engagement product, produced customer satisfaction gains measured in hundreds of basis points, voice direct contribution gains measured in hundreds of basis points, and loyalty enrollment growth measured in hundreds of basis points. These are real commercial outcomes from early infrastructure investment.

Their app experience, offering a graphical interface with scrollable inventory and map navigation, was weeks away from going live at the time of the forum. When that app connects into ChatGPT, Mike Mahar described what he expects will happen as explosive.

The Answer About Data Quality

Jason Cincotta of Kismet called low-quality AI-generated content “agentic slop.” His point was direct: generating a batch of blog posts about a property and hoping they confuse an LLM into recommending it will fail. LLMs see through content volume. They evaluate quality, accuracy, and consistency. A gap between what a hotel’s marketing copy promises and what the property’s factual data actually describes will eventually produce recommendations that fail to convert, and may harm the property’s standing in AI answers over time.

What works is honest, specific, factually accurate data, organized so it can be transported into other interfaces. Location coordinates. Google Places IDs. Room attribute descriptions that mean something to a semantic search. Specific amenity details. The aspirational language that has always made hospitality marketing feel evocative falls flat when an AI tries to answer a specific question about a specific property.

Cincotta’s team spent months building the groundwork for one client to appear in ChatGPT recommendations for Napa Valley golf travel. The result was an interaction where the AI passed contextual information from the conversation into the booking flow: dates the traveler had mentioned, preferences stated earlier, the specific intent around golf. That is what a quality implementation produces. It took months of structured work to get there.

Visibility Requires Work: The Five Channels That Generate It

Connecting an app leaves a hotel far from instantly discoverable inside ChatGPT or Gemini. Mike Mahar compared it to enabling an Alexa skill. The infrastructure exists. The data flows. The user still needs to find and activate the connection.

Brewer laid out the five channels through which a hotel can generate traffic from a ChatGPT results surface. Apps are one. Listings, citations, and sources are three more. Paid placements will eventually be a fifth. Hotels are waiting for app infrastructure to mature. The citation and source channels are generating real revenue today.

Brewer cited boutique properties generating three to five thousand dollars per month through good SEO producing citations and source appearances in AI responses. Large multi-location chains are running at millions of dollars annually through those same three channels, before app infrastructure is even in play. The brands that understand what conversation patterns matter and what content structures support those patterns are already winning.

GEO, Generative Engine Optimization, is the strategic layer above traditional SEO. Mahar described Wyndham’s upcoming e-commerce platform as being designed for LLM exposure from the outset. The architecture of the platform treats machine readability as a first-class requirement. That is a meaningfully different approach than adding structured data to a platform that was never designed for it.

One channel that surprised the room: LinkedIn. Brewer’s recommendation was specific. Because LinkedIn is treated as an authoritative source by major LLMs, posting detailed, factual hotel content through a property’s company page feeds directly into AI answer assembly. Post the way brands once used Twitter: high frequency, factual detail, consistent presence. LinkedIn posts get picked up and surfaced in AI responses. It is an open channel that the overwhelming majority of hotel marketing teams have overlooked entirely.

The Surprising Advantage Independent Hotels Have Right Now

Independent hotels have structural advantages in AI visibility that branded properties cannot easily replicate. Ira Vouk made this case in session two, and it deserves more attention than it gets.

An independent hotel controls its own domain entirely. It can publish detailed, specific, factual answers to questions that OTA template listings and brand-standard websites never address. The question about whether the third-floor rooms face the street or the courtyard. The question about whether the parking garage clears a high-profile vehicle. The question about which room has the best view at sunset.

AI systems surface and reward this specificity. The independent operator who has taken the time to publish accurate, factual answers to specific questions is more likely to appear when a traveler asks those specific questions. OTAs have been ahead of hotels in traditional SEO for years. The gap in AI search is newer and narrower. The independent who moves now establishes presence before the field consolidates.

Payments, Legal Responsibility, and Governance

–

The Payment Infrastructure Is Ready

The technical infrastructure required to process agentic transactions already exists. Andrew Beckmann of Stripe confirmed that Stripe and major merchant service providers can handle remote payment flows that require zero human form-completion, including transactions where no physical card is ever presented.

OpenAI’s announcement that it would expose commerce capabilities inside ChatGPT apps answered the merchant-of-record question that had been blocking many brands from moving forward. The hotel, through its app, is the merchant. That clarity matters commercially. It defines who owns the transaction, who takes on the compliance obligations, and who controls the customer relationship through the booking.

Beckmann described Stripe’s digital wallet, Link, used by more than 200 million people, as infrastructure that carries more than payment capability. It carries preference history, past booking behavior, and eventually loyalty context. A traveler booking through an AI agent on a third-party platform could have their preferences and history travel with them through the payment layer. That vision, payments as personalization infrastructure rather than purely transaction infrastructure, is worth watching as the space develops.

Legal Responsibility Is Clear and Unambiguous

Greg Duff of Foster Garvey PC is the kind of voice every panel needs. He said it plainly: when an AI agent acts on a hotel’s behalf, the hotel is responsible for what it does. Full stop.

The Air Canada case is the precedent Duff cited. Air Canada attempted to disclaim responsibility for incorrect information their chatbot provided to passengers. Courts held Air Canada responsible. The chatbot was an extension of the brand. Its statements carried the same weight as statements from a human representative.

That precedent applies to every scenario on Ira Vouk’s three-tier framework. Whether the AI is a chatbot on a hotel website, an embedded booking agent, or a fully automated A-to-A system, the hotelier owns the pricing accuracy, availability accuracy, privacy compliance, and payment compliance that the AI system delivers. Treating AI output as a separate entity with its own accountability is a posture courts are unlikely to accept.

Brewer and Duff both pointed to the same governance anchor: connect AI systems back to the single source of truth, the PMS and the CRS. Bookings still flow through existing reservation infrastructure. The pathway to completion now runs through a different interface. The underlying accountability holds firm.

Measuring Visibility and Getting to a Baseline

–

The Measurement Challenge Is Real but Solvable

The panel addressed measurement honestly. Traditional SEO has established metrics: traffic, click-through rates, ranking positions. AI search optimization has yet to reach equivalent consensus around what to measure or how.

Ira Vouk’s recommendation was direct. Establish a baseline now, before implementing any strategy, so that progress can be documented as the field develops. Free tools exist for initial visibility assessment. The industry will converge on standardized metrics over time. A hotel organization that has no baseline established today will have no way to demonstrate progress when that standardization arrives.

The approach I describe in my book, Invisible: What to Do When AI Erases Your Business (available on Amazon), involves running a structured set of prompts relevant to a specific property across AI platforms over several weeks. These prompts deliberately avoid using the hotel’s domain name. They describe the property through location, category, attributes, and proximity. The data returned is probabilistic rather than deterministic. Trends become visible. Competitive gaps emerge. Where a competitor appears instead of your property points toward what that competitor has done structurally that your property has left undone.

Here is something most hotel analytics teams have missed entirely. When AI agents visit a hotel’s website, they identify themselves at the server level. GA4 is blind to this. Google Tag Manager is blind to this. Standard analytics infrastructure misses it entirely.

One small hotel the Kismet team serves receives six thousand agent visits per month. Those visits are entirely invisible to the hotel’s current analytics. Within that traffic, some agents are training future model outputs about the property. Others are actively retrieving information to serve a traveler query happening right now. Understanding which agents are visiting, what pages they access, and what their purpose is requires server-level visibility tools. The gap between what hotels think is happening on their websites and what is actually happening is larger than most teams realize.

What the Full Forum Made Clear

The Destination AI Forum covered a wide range of ground, but the through-line across both sessions was consistent.

This is a structural shift in how hotel products get found, evaluated, and purchased. The channel is changing. The interface is changing. The commercial model is changing. Hotels that wait for the picture to become clearer before acting are making a choice. That choice has a compounding cost.

What I find genuinely exciting about this moment is that the field remains wide open. The standards are forming. The infrastructure is being built. A hotel organization that invests in data quality, structured content, and MCP connectivity now is building ahead of a trend, pouring foundation while the concrete is still affordable and the field is still forgiving.

The independent hotel that answers every specific guest question on a well-structured, machine-readable website is doing the same essential thing as an enterprise brand connecting first-party data through MCP into public LLMs. Both are making sure AI can find them, describe them accurately, and point travelers toward a place where a booking can actually happen.

That is the work. The window is open. The brands that move through it now will look back at this moment as the one that mattered.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is AI-driven hotel booking, and why does it matter now?

Short answer: AI platforms are replacing traditional search as the primary channel through which travelers discover and evaluate hotels.

Elaboration: Travelers are using conversational AI tools including ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity to research destinations, compare options, and select properties. These platforms return a curated shortlist, never a wall of links for a traveler to evaluate independently. They assemble recommendations based on stated preferences and the full context of the conversation. A hotel absent from AI-generated recommendations loses consideration before the traveler ever visits a website. Phocuswright research finds that 40% of U.S. travelers now use generative AI for trip planning. OpenAI recently reported 900 million weekly active users globally, with accelerating growth across Germany, India, and the Asia-Pacific region.

Q: What is MCP, and does my hotel actually need it?

Short answer: MCP, or Model Context Protocol, is the technical standard that connects a hotel’s real-time data directly to an AI language model.

Elaboration: Without an MCP connection, an LLM answering a query about a hotel retrieves whatever it can access from web sources. That typically means OTA listings, review site data, and potentially outdated descriptions, and it frequently means the AI recommends booking through an OTA. With a quality MCP solution in place, the same query returns accurate first-party data, real-time availability, current pricing, and a direct booking path. The barrier is executional, full stop. Building a quality MCP solution requires real technical investment. Hotels that treat it as a quick configuration task will receive results that reflect that approach.

Q: How far away is agent-to-agent booking, and should hotels prepare for it now?

Short answer: The thirty-six-month timeline Brad Brewer described means the window for foundational infrastructure investment is open right now.

Elaboration: Agent-to-agent commerce, where an AI agent books accommodation on behalf of a traveler without human involvement, is closer than most hotel executives expect. Unmanaged business travel is the most likely first application at scale. A company’s AI agent receives a trip requirement, broadcasts it across properties with the right technical infrastructure, and confirms a booking. Properties without that infrastructure are invisible to the transaction entirely. Building infrastructure before the volume arrives is substantially less costly than building infrastructure after a competitor has already captured the channel.

Q: What is the difference between SEO, GEO, and AI visibility for hotels?

Short answer: SEO helps travelers find you on Google. GEO helps AI models learn about you during training. AI visibility determines whether you appear in real-time AI-generated responses.

Elaboration: These three layers work together and reinforce each other. SEO provides the foundation: technical health, content quality, structured data, and authoritative inbound links. GEO, Generative Engine Optimization, is about being present in the authoritative sources that AI models train on, including trade press coverage, Wikipedia, and well-cited third-party content. AI visibility is what happens in real time: whether a Perplexity search or a ChatGPT conversation surfaces a property, and whether the description it produces is accurate. A hotel can have strong traditional SEO and still be invisible in AI answers if the GEO and real-time visibility layers have been neglected.

Q: Should independent hotels approach AI visibility differently than branded properties?

Short answer: Independent hotels have structural advantages that branded properties cannot easily replicate, particularly around content specificity and domain control.

Elaboration: A branded hotel operates within a template. Its website reflects a brand’s standard content architecture, which limits how specific and granular the individual property’s content can be. An independent hotel owns its domain entirely and can publish highly specific, factual content that answers exact questions a traveler would ask: accessibility details, neighborhood characteristics, what a specific room’s view looks like, what the noise environment is on different floors. AI systems surface and reward this specificity. OTAs have been ahead of hotels in traditional SEO for years. The gap in AI search is newer. Independent operators who build structured, specific content now establish presence before the field consolidates around OTA dominance again.

Q: What should a revenue manager do right now to prepare for AI-driven booking?

Short answer: Test what AI platforms currently say about a property, then address the gaps in structured data, content accuracy, and direct booking path.

Elaboration: Start by running a series of prompts about a property across ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews. Include prompts that describe the property through location, category, and attributes without using the property name. Document what appears. Note where competitors appear instead. Then work backward: is the gap technical (structured data, schema markup), content-based (missing factual information, inaccurate descriptions), or commercial (a booking path that fails when an agent tries to complete it)? Revenue managers who build this testing into their regular workflow now will have a documented baseline from which to measure progress as the measurement standards for AI visibility mature.

Q: Who is legally responsible when an AI agent makes a booking error?

Short answer: The hotel is responsible for what its AI systems do.

Elaboration: Greg Duff of Foster Garvey PC made this point clearly and the Air Canada legal case supports it. When an AI agent acts as an extension of a hotel’s commercial operation, whether it is a chatbot on the website, an embedded booking agent, or a fully automated transaction system, the hotel owns the outcome. Pricing accuracy, availability accuracy, privacy compliance, and payment compliance all remain the hotel’s responsibility regardless of the technology intermediating the interaction. Governance frameworks should connect AI systems back to the authoritative data sources, the PMS and the CRS, and treat AI output as an extension of the hotel brand rather than as a separate operating entity.

Q: How do payments work in an agentic booking scenario?

Short answer: The payment infrastructure for agentic transactions already exists. The primary challenge is connecting hotel inventory to a surface where those transactions can be completed.

Elaboration: Stripe and major merchant service providers have the technical capacity to process agentic transactions, including flows that require zero human form-completion. OpenAI’s announcement that it would expose commerce inside ChatGPT apps answered the merchant-of-record question: the hotel, through its app, is the merchant. Stripe’s digital wallet, Link, used by more than 200 million people, carries preference history and potentially loyalty context that can travel with a traveler through a booking even when that booking originates from a third-party AI interface. The operational risk lives on the hotel side: whether the hotel’s app and data infrastructure are solid enough to enable and complete the transaction accurately.

Q: What are the most common blind spots hotel executives have about AI right now?

Short answer: Three blind spots dominated the panel’s closing remarks: the belief that a website is still the center of digital strategy, the assumption that AI visibility can be addressed later, and an underestimation of the technical complexity involved.

Elaboration: Brad Brewer said it plainly: AI assembles answers from structured data. Browsing websites is foreign to how it works. A hotel with excellent website design and poor structured data is invisible to the channel increasingly driving discovery. Mike Mahar identified the “wait and see” posture as the most common and costly response he encounters. Every month of delay in connecting to AI platforms is a month of learning, feedback data, and competitive positioning that competitors accumulate and keep. Jason Cincotta addressed the technical dimension: assuming visibility is a matter of adjusting website settings misunderstands how AI discovery systems actually work. It requires technology adoption, structured data organization, and ongoing maintenance.

Q: What does this shift mean for how hotels should think about direct booking strategy?

Short answer: Direct booking now requires presence in the places where travelers make decisions. That increasingly means inside AI platforms, ahead of hotel websites.

Elaboration: The direct booking model of the previous decade assumed a traveler would eventually arrive at a hotel website and that a well-designed booking engine would convert them. AI platforms are capturing the decision before that journey begins. When ChatGPT recommends a property, a meaningful portion of travelers book based on that recommendation without conducting additional independent research. The hotel that appears in that recommendation, with an MCP-connected app providing accurate data and a direct booking path with minimal friction, captures revenue that previously required a traveler to navigate through multiple intermediaries. Distribution is moving. Direct booking strategy needs to move with it, ahead of the channel rather than reacting to it.
