How AI Travel Planners Find Your Hotel: 7 Checks for Hotel Teams

Michael J. Goldrich · September 30, 2026

By Michael J. Goldrich · Published September 30, 2026

A family asks an AI travel planner for a hotel near their child’s tournament. They need room for five, parking, and a rate they can cancel if the schedule changes. Your hotel might be a good fit. But can the planner find enough reliable information to explain why?

That is a useful place for a hotel team to start. AI travel planners can use information from travel platforms, websites, and the traveler’s own request. They do not all use the same sources or choose hotels in the same way. There is no public formula that tells us exactly how every recommendation is made.

What you can check is whether the information about your hotel helps someone make a sound choice, and whether the booking link lets them act on it. These seven checks give your team a practical starting point.

1. Make it clear who the hotel is a good fit for

“Perfect for every traveler” sounds welcoming, but it gives people very little to work with. A couple looking for a quiet weekend needs different details from a family traveling for a tournament.

Describe the features that matter to those guests: room arrangements, workspaces, adult-oriented areas, or access to nearby activities. Be equally clear about limitations. If the pool closes for part of the year, say when.

Try this: Choose one common guest request. Read the relevant page on your website and ask whether someone unfamiliar with the hotel could tell if it meets that need.

2. Explain the location the way a guest would use it

“Minutes from the stadium” leaves a lot unanswered. Is that a walk, a drive, or a train ride? A hotel can look close on a map and still be inconvenient to reach from the place a guest needs to go.

Keep the address consistent across your website and major listings. Give useful directions to the destinations your guests care about. When you include travel times, name the mode of transport and make clear that conditions can change.

Try this: Compare your directions page with your main listings. Look for claims that sound convenient but leave a guest guessing about the actual journey.

3. Say exactly who can stay in each room

A family asks, “Can the five of us stay together?” Your booking engine may answer with a room name such as “Family Deluxe.” Those are not necessarily the same answer.

State the maximum occupancy, bed configuration, rules for children, and conditions for extra beds. If connecting rooms are available only by request, explain that clearly rather than implying they are guaranteed.

Try this: Ask your reservations team which room questions they answer repeatedly. Check whether the website answers those questions just as clearly.

Even a suitable room needs to be available for the exact dates and party. The guide to AI travel planner booking explains the difference between a suggested room and a reservation.

4. Remove surprises from the policies

A parking charge, a pet restriction, or a nonrefundable rate can change a guest’s decision. These details deserve the same care as the photographs and room descriptions.

Check parking, pet rules, check-in times, required fees, and cancellation terms. Be specific about which policy applies to which rate. A flexible cancellation option does not make every booking refundable.

Try this: Compare the policies on your website with the external listings your team manages. Decide who maintains the approved version and who corrects each listing when something changes. Put dates on temporary offers.

5. Read the outside sources that appear in AI answers

Your website may tell one story while an old review, directory entry, or travel article tells another. When an AI answer links to an outside page, open it and read what it actually says about the hotel.

Does it describe a service you no longer offer? Does it confuse your property with another? Does it include a useful detail that your own website leaves out?

Try this: Save the relevant links from an answer to a realistic guest question. Check the hotel’s identity, facilities, and policies on those pages. Correct factual errors where you can, and request corrections elsewhere through the platform’s normal process.

The citation-source research guide explains why the sources an assistant uses need to be considered in context. A familiar website name alone does not explain why a particular page appeared.

6. Follow the booking link yourself

Imagine the family finds your hotel in an AI answer and clicks the booking link. Does it take them to the right property with their dates and party size intact? Or do they land on a homepage and have to start again?

That transfer from a recommendation to a booking page is often called the “booking handoff.” The practical question is simple: can the guest continue without losing the details that made the hotel a suitable choice?

Try this: Follow the same link a traveler would receive. Check the property, dates, number of guests, and room or rate where the system supports carrying those details through. If the option is unavailable, the page should make that clear.

A recommendation alone does not reserve a room. The ten-point booking readiness checklist helps your team review the next steps in more detail.

7. Repeat the questions and keep the answers

One good AI answer is encouraging. One poor answer is worth investigating. Neither tells you how consistently your hotel appears.

Keep a small set of realistic guest questions and ask them again over time. Save the full answers and source links, along with the platform, date, and trip details used. If you change the question or the way you test, record that too.

Try this: Separate three observations: the answer names your hotel, links to your website, or recommends your hotel with a reason. Those tell you different things. The AI visibility measurement guide explains how to distinguish them.

Start with one guest question and one correction

You do not need to rewrite the whole website to begin. Choose a guest question that matters to your hotel, work through these checks, and turn what you find into a short list of specific fixes.

For each fix, save the evidence, name the person who can make the change, and decide how you will check it afterward. Reservations may need to clarify occupancy. Marketing may need to update a page. Distribution may need to correct a listing. Your booking provider may need to investigate a broken link or missing trip details.

Start with errors that could lead someone to book the wrong thing or give up. Then improve the information that explains where your hotel is genuinely a good fit.

Clearer information and a working booking path help guests make better-informed decisions. They do not guarantee that an AI planner will recommend your hotel. They do give your team concrete work to do, and a way to check whether it was done.

Frequently asked questions

Do all AI travel planners choose hotels the same way?

No. They can use different travel inventory, web sources, and information supplied by the traveler. There is no public selection formula that applies to every planner.

What should a hotel check first?

Start with a common guest need. Check whether your room details, location, and policies answer it accurately, then follow the booking link to see whether the guest can continue.

Will correcting our information guarantee an AI recommendation?

No. It improves the information available to guests and the systems helping them, but it does not guarantee a recommendation or a booking.
