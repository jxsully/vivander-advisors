We Each Get a Genie. We Still Own the Wish.

Michael J. Goldrich · November 5, 2026

Aladdin needs food. He rubs the lamp, and the genie provides it.

Then the requests grow. Fine clothes. Wealth. Servants. Eventually, a magnificent palace appears where there had been empty ground.

Imagine waking up to that.

Something that should have taken years stands finished by morning. Aladdin has gained access to a capability far beyond anything he could accomplish alone.

The wishes work. His life changes because he makes them.

That is the attraction of the lamp. A person can describe what they want and ask something extraordinarily capable to make it happen.

Personal AI agents put a genie within reach

Personal AI agents bring a version of that promise into our working lives.

Think of ChatGPT Dot, Claude Duet, Meta Muse, and Instinct. These are part of a growing category of personal agents built around the possibility of doing work on our behalf.

The request becomes an assignment. With the appropriate tools and permissions, an agent can move through several steps toward an outcome.

“Prepare me for tomorrow’s meeting.”

“Find the information we need for this proposal.”

“Help coordinate the project.”

For someone running a business, managing a team, or simply trying to finish the day’s work, that can feel like discovering a lamp.

A task that would have consumed the afternoon is ready for review before lunch. An idea that kept getting postponed becomes something you can finally attempt.

The possibilities deserve our attention.

So does Aladdin.

He receives extraordinary power before he has much experience managing it. Each successful wish gives him another reason to believe he knows how the lamp works.

He knows how to summon the genie. He knows how to ask.

But has he thought through who should have access? What other people need to understand? What could happen if that capability follows someone else’s instructions?

The magician understands what the lamp can do

In the story, those questions arrive wearing the clothes of a merchant.

“New lamps for old.”

While Aladdin is away, the magician offers to exchange worn lamps for clean new ones. The princess does not know the old lamp’s importance. Aladdin has never explained it to her.

The exchange seems harmless.

The magician takes possession of the lamp, summons the genie, and orders the palace carried away, with the princess inside.

Aladdin returns to find that the life he built has disappeared.

For our purposes, the magician represents everything that can go wrong when powerful capability meets poorly understood access.

A private document shared with the wrong recipient. Proprietary information copied somewhere it should never have gone. A malicious instruction hidden inside material an agent has been asked to read. Permission granted for a small task that allows much broader actions.

The offer may look as ordinary as a new lamp.

Connect this account. Open this document. Enable this integration. Give the agent access so it can finish the job.

Each step can seem reasonable on its own. The consequences depend on what that access makes possible.

The magician understands the lamp’s authority. The people living in the palace do not.

That is the imbalance that makes the theft possible.

At work, a similar imbalance can develop gradually. We connect the agent to our information, watch it complete useful tasks, and give it more responsibility. As the results improve, we spend less time reviewing.

Eventually, “take care of this task” becomes “make whatever decisions are necessary.”

That is a much larger wish.

Human in the loop and human on the loop

Consider an agent handling customer requests.

At first, it drafts replies for approval. A person checks the facts, considers the relationship, and decides what to send.

The human is in the loop. The agent prepares the action; the person authorizes it.

Later, the agent might receive permission to send routine replies within agreed limits. It can answer delivery questions, retrieve order details, and explain an established policy. Refund exceptions and unusual commitments still require review.

The human is on the loop. The agent can act, while a person supervises, checks results, and remains able to intervene.

Both arrangements allow meaningful delegation.

Neither removes the need for someone to own what happens.

The difficulty begins when we stop making those distinctions. We no longer know which decisions the agent is making, what information it is using, or which commitments it has created.

Then a customer challenges a promise, a confidential file reaches the wrong person, or an employee discovers a decision they cannot explain.

“The agent did it” offers very little help to the person affected.

Someone gave it access. Someone defined its assignment. Someone decided which actions could proceed without approval.

Those are human decisions too.

We still own the wish

In the traditional story of Aladdin and the Magic Lamp, Aladdin takes an active role in recovering the lamp. He finds the princess, works with her, and regains what he lost.

Imagine the conversation they should have after the palace returns.

Who may use the lamp? What may they ask it to do? Which wishes require a conversation first? How will they recognize when something has gone wrong?

There will be more wishes. The lamp remains valuable.

What changes is their understanding of the responsibility that comes with it.

We are entering a period when more people will have access to something resembling a genie. Our ambitions can grow. Work that once required resources beyond our reach may become possible.

We can be the delegator. We can give an agent authority to make routine decisions. We can let it carry substantial work forward.

But we still own the decision to grant that authority, the boundaries around it, and the consequences of actions taken on our behalf.

The trouble begins when we treat delegation as permission to stop caring what happens next.

Each of us may get a genie.

We still have to own the wish.
