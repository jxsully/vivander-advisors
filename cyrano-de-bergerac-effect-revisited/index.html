The Cyrano de Bergerac Effect Revisited: Can You Defend the Intelligence You Present?

Michael J. Goldrich · November 1, 2026

The presentation is excellent. The recommendation is clear. Every objection appears to have an answer.

Then someone asks a question that was not in the brief.

What happens if the assumption is wrong? Why did you reject the other option? Which part of this recommendation would you refuse to automate?

That is where I would look for the Cyrano de Bergerac Effect today.

On July 9, 2025, I wrote about the gap between an AI-enhanced online persona and the person who shows up without that assistance. The analogy came from Cyrano supplying the words that Christian could not produce himself. My concern was what happens when polished communication creates an impression of expertise that the person cannot sustain.

Since then, research has explored related questions in dating, communication and learning. Two later publications explicitly use the term “Cyrano effect.” They give us useful evidence.

The update I would make is this: the most important gap is between the intelligence we present and the judgment we can actually exercise.

What the research adds

Better words can make a better first impression

David J. Roaché, Liesel L. Sharabi, Lindsey Hicks and McKenzie M. Hurley studied 168 adults assigned to write a date-request message themselves, use AI, or modify an AI-generated message. Independent judges rated AI-generated messages more favorably on effectiveness, affiliation and explicitness. Participants' own quality ratings did not differ by source.

Their study was published online in September 2025, although an earlier version had been presented in July 2024. It measures message quality, not whether an eventual relationship succeeds or a person becomes more capable.

For business leaders, that distinction matters. An excellent piece of communication can be genuinely useful while telling us less than we assume about the person behind it.

The person sending the message and the person receiving it may see different things

Lennart Ante's 2026 study in Telematics and Informatics examined AI-assisted dating through 45 interviews. It identified tensions between users who felt AI helped express their authentic selves, recipients who felt deceived, and the anxiety of moving from polished messages to an unassisted meeting.

These are qualitative accounts, not a population estimate or proof that AI caused every disappointing encounter. Still, they sharpen the question: what did each person believe the communication represented?

A colleague may think, “This tool helps me express what I mean.” A client may think, “These words demonstrate your expertise.” Both interpretations can exist until a decision exposes the difference.

Finishing the task does not guarantee learning it

In How AI Impacts Skill Formation, Judy Hanwen Shen and Alex Tamkin studied developers learning an unfamiliar Python library. In the main randomized experiment with 52 participants, the AI-assisted group performed worse on the subsequent knowledge assessment, without a statistically significant overall speed advantage. Their exploratory analysis also found stronger learning among some users who stayed engaged through conceptual questions and explanations.

This is a narrow, short-term coding study, initially released as a preprint in January 2026. It does not establish that AI use generally erodes intelligence. It does give managers a reason to evaluate learning separately from completed output.

What I would refine in my original argument

The phrase “in-person dunce” made the original headline memorable. It also risks pointing us toward the wrong test.

A hesitant speaker can have excellent judgment. A fluent presenter can have very little. Someone working in a second language may use AI to communicate expertise they already possess. Someone with a communication disability may need assistance to participate fully.

We should be careful about turning charisma, speed or unaided speaking into a proxy for competence.

My concern is narrower and more consequential: a person presents work as something they understand and can take responsibility for, but cannot explain its reasoning, recognize its limitations or adapt it when conditions change.

That gap can appear in an email exchange just as easily as in a meeting room. It can remain hidden even when the final recommendation happens to be correct.

Consider a hotel commercial team reviewing an AI-assisted pricing proposal. The document recommends raising rates around an upcoming event. It has charts, market language and a convincing executive summary.

Before approving it, I would want the person responsible to explain which demand signals support the increase, which costs and constraints were included, and what would trigger a reversal. If a key assumption changes, can the team revise the recommendation intelligently?

The document is useful. Ownership of the decision requires more.

A practical test for accountable AI use

I would build four questions into the review of consequential AI-assisted work.

1. Can you explain it?

Walk through the recommendation in your own words. Identify the evidence and assumptions. Explain why the chosen approach fits this situation. Looking up a detail is fine; repeatedly returning to an AI answer without understanding it is a warning sign.

2. Can you challenge it?

Name the strongest reason the recommendation might fail. Show what you checked and what remains uncertain. A person who can explain a limitation is often more useful than one who delivers uninterrupted confidence.

3. Can you adapt it?

Change an important condition: the budget, the deadline, the customer need or the available data. Ask how the recommendation should change and why. This should be a relevant working discussion, with appropriate accommodations, rather than a surprise performance designed to embarrass someone.

4. Will you own it?

Make clear who checked the sources, who approves the action and who will respond if it goes wrong. “The AI said so” cannot be the final explanation for a business decision.

These questions assess responsible use of a powerful tool. They also make it easier to identify where additional training or review is needed.

Give people a reason to learn, not just look capable

Leaders help create this problem when they reward polished output without asking how it was produced or understood.

If speed is the only visible measure, taking time to investigate an assumption can look like underperformance. If employees are encouraged to use AI but treated as less capable when they acknowledge it, concealment becomes an understandable temptation.

Set expectations before the work begins. Explain which uses are welcome, which require disclosure, which information must stay out of the tool, and which decisions require human review. Apply those expectations consistently, including to senior leaders.

Then make learning part of the workflow. After using AI to prepare a recommendation, ask the employee to explain it, examine an alternative and identify a mistake the tool could have made. Give them time to resolve what they do not yet understand.

My original article encouraged people to turn borrowed information into genuine expertise. I would make that advice more demanding today: the evidence of learning is what you can do with the material, not how sophisticated the summary sounds.

AI can help us produce stronger work. It can also make it easier to overlook the understanding that strong work requires.

Before you sign the proposal, deliver the presentation or approve the recommendation, ask yourself one question:

Can I explain, challenge and take responsibility for what I am putting my name on?

That is the test I would carry forward from Cyrano.
