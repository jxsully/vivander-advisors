Two Simple Boxes Reveal What We Miss About AI

Michael J. Goldrich · November 3, 2026

An AI-written apology can promise a refund your hotel cannot offer. It can sound thoughtful, polished and completely appropriate. Generative AI and personal agents make that gap between a convincing answer and dependable work worth examining.

Imagine two boxes on a desk. One is labeled IN. The other is labeled OUT.

You put a question into the first box. A useful answer appears in the second. You try another question. Another convincing answer comes back.

After a few exchanges, you might reasonably say, “That’s intelligent.”

But what have you actually observed? You’ve seen the quality of the output. You haven’t established how the answer was produced, whether it’s accurate, or what the system can reliably do next.

That distinction is a useful starting point for understanding generative AI. It becomes even more important when we give AI permission to act.

The Chinese Room: what can an answer prove?

Our two-box illustration borrows from philosopher John Searle’s Chinese Room thought experiment.

Imagine someone inside a room who cannot read Chinese. Questions written in Chinese arrive through an opening. The person follows an elaborate rulebook, matching symbols and producing responses that go back outside.

To a Chinese-speaking observer, the responses could look like evidence that someone inside understands the language. Yet the person following the instructions does not.

Searle used this scenario to argue that following a computer program is not, by itself, sufficient for understanding. The IN and OUT boxes are a visual shorthand for the exchange, not a literal detail of his original setup. You can read Searle’s original argument.

His conclusion remains disputed. AI researcher John McCarthy, for example, challenged the argument that the person’s lack of understanding tells us everything about the capabilities of the whole system.

We don’t need to settle that debate to use the illustration. Its practical value is the question it encourages: What should we infer from an impressive response?

Generative AI: what happens between IN and OUT?

Modern generative AI does not contain a person consulting a handwritten rulebook.

A language model uses numerical parameters learned during training. It processes your prompt and the context available to it, then generates text in small pieces called tokens. Each new piece depends on the preceding context. This learned process is much more flexible than looking up a fixed answer, but it can also produce plausible statements that are wrong.

The boxes help us picture the interaction:

  • IN: Your request, relevant information, examples and constraints.
  • BETWEEN: A trained model processing that context.
  • OUT: A generated response, such as a draft, explanation, summary or recommendation.

This is a simplified picture, not a technical diagram of every AI system.

Suppose you put “Write a response to an unhappy hotel guest” into the IN box. You might receive a polished apology.

But the model may not have the hotel’s refund policy, the guest’s booking details or the history of the complaint. A response can sound appropriate while promising something the hotel cannot provide.

Add those details, and you give the system a better basis for its draft. Review the result, and you can catch an unsupported promise before it reaches the guest.

That’s where your contribution matters: supplying the situation and evaluating what comes back.

That habit of supplying context and checking results is central to practical AI literacy and training. To assess it in real work, see how to measure employee AI fluency.

Personal AI agents connect the boxes to the world

Now imagine that the output can become an instruction to another tool.

An AI agent might search a document folder, inspect a calendar or retrieve a booking record. The tool’s result becomes fresh input. The agent evaluates what happened and decides on another step.

The interaction becomes a loop: receive information, choose an action, inspect the result and continue.

This ability to use tools and adapt subsequent steps is central to agentic systems. It creates opportunities for errors to accumulate, too, which makes stopping conditions and human oversight important. Anthropic’s guide to building agents explains this distinction between workflows and agents.

Consider a personal agent preparing you for tomorrow’s client meeting. With appropriate access, it could locate the agenda, review relevant documents, identify outstanding questions and assemble a briefing.

Its usefulness depends on more than the quality of its writing. Did it find the right client? Did it use the latest document? Did it distinguish a proposed deadline from an agreed one?

A convincing paragraph cannot answer those questions by itself. You need evidence from the work.

How to use generative AI and personal agents with judgment

The two-box model encourages you to take responsibility for the full exchange.

Before the work begins, define what belongs in the input box. Before accepting the output, decide how you’ll judge it. Before enabling an agent to act, establish what it may do and when it must stop.

You can be ambitious about the help you request while being specific about the boundaries.

“Help me get ready for the meeting” leaves many choices open.

“Review these three documents, identify unresolved decisions, and prepare a one-page briefing with source links. Flag missing information. Do not change shared files” gives the system a task you can evaluate.

Here are five ways to put that understanding into practice.

1. Give it the context that changes the answer.

Include the audience, goal, relevant facts and constraints. Share only what the task requires.

For example: “Draft a response to this guest using the attached refund policy. Keep it under 150 words. Flag any request the policy does not address.”

2. Define what a successful output looks like.

Specify the deliverable and the standard it must meet.

For example: “Compare these three proposals on total cost, implementation time and support. Identify missing information rather than filling gaps with assumptions.”

Now you have something concrete to check.

3. Ask for evidence and verify what matters.

Request source links, supporting passages or calculations. Open the sources and confirm they support the claims.

A generated citation can also be wrong. Match the depth of your review to the consequences of an error.

4. Separate preparation from permission to act.

Let an agent gather information and prepare options before allowing consequential changes. Use available access controls and approval settings to enforce boundaries.

For example: “Find three suitable meeting times and prepare invitation text. I will approve the time and send the invitation.”

5. Expand responsibility after demonstrated reliability.

Start with a limited task you can inspect. Correct errors and clarify expectations before broadening the assignment.

For example, review several meeting briefings before relying on an agent for more complex preparation. Look for accurate sources, useful prioritization and honest reporting when something could not be completed.

The two boxes are simple. Using them well requires judgment.

You choose what goes in, assess what comes out and control what happens next. That’s how an impressive AI response becomes useful, dependable assistance.

Questions about generative AI and personal agents

What is the difference between generative AI and a personal AI agent?

Generative AI produces content from a prompt and available context. A personal AI agent can use a model with connected tools to take steps toward a goal, inspect results and decide what to do next.

Does the Chinese Room prove that modern AI cannot understand?

No. The Chinese Room is a disputed philosophical argument about whether running a program is sufficient for understanding. It does not settle how modern AI should be evaluated for a particular task.

How can I make AI-generated answers more dependable?

Provide relevant facts and constraints, define the expected output, ask for supporting evidence and check important claims against the original sources. A fluent answer or citation can still be wrong.

What should I let my first personal AI agent do?

Start with a limited task you can inspect, such as preparing a meeting briefing from selected documents. Define which tools it may use, what it may change and when it must stop for review.
